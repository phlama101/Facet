## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Building the world's longest calendar"
**Hook:** The geologic timescale is one of science's greatest collaborative achievements — a calendar stretching 4,567 million years, built incrementally by geologists on six continents over two centuries. It began as a purely relative sequence: Cambrian over Precambrian, Cretaceous over Jurassic, each name attached to a place where that sequence of rocks was first described. It was calibrated with numbers only in the twentieth century, as radiometric dating matured. Today the International Commission on Stratigraphy (ICS) maintains the timescale through a formal treaty-like process, with specific rock outcrops in Spain, China, the UK, and elsewhere serving as the globally agreed reference points for each period boundary. The timescale is not finished: every few years, the ICS publishes a revised chart with updated boundary ages as new radiometric dates supersede older ones.

**Key terms:** geologic timescale, eon, era, period, epoch, stage, GSSP (Global Boundary Stratotype Section and Point), biostratigraphy, chemostratigraphy, magnetostratigraphy, Phanerozoic.

### Section 2 — Concept: "From layers to numbers: how the timescale is built"

#### The time/rock hierarchy
- Time units: eon > era > period > epoch > age (ICS formal terminology)
- Rock units (time-rock / chronostratigraphic): eonothem > erathem > system > series > stage
- The same boundary — e.g., the Cretaceous-Paleogene — is both a time boundary (end of the Cretaceous Period, 66.0 Ma) and a rock boundary (base of the Paleogene System)
- Phanerozoic eon: "visible life" — spans 538.8 Ma to present; subdivided into Paleozoic, Mesozoic, Cenozoic eras
- Precambrian: informal term for the ~88% of Earth history before the Phanerozoic; formally divided into Hadean, Archean, Proterozoic eons

#### How boundaries are defined: the GSSP system
- Before GSSPs: each country used its own definition of period boundaries → correlation chaos
- GSSP (Golden Spike): a specific physical horizon in a specific rock outcrop, formally ratified by the ICS and IUGS, that serves as the global reference point for the base of a given stage, series, or system
- Selected on the basis of: (1) continuous deposition across the boundary; (2) presence of biostratigraphic markers (typically a first appearance datum of an index fossil); (3) geochemical or paleomagnetic signals for correlation; (4) good radiometric dating of associated volcanic ash beds or intrusions; (5) global accessibility
- Example: The Cretaceous-Paleogene GSSP is at El Kef, Tunisia — a continuous marine section with the iridium anomaly, δ¹³C excursion, and dramatic change in calcareous nannofossils at exactly the same level
- Once ratified, the GSSP defines the boundary in rock; the absolute age is then derived from nearby datable material (usually a volcanic ash layer within ±1–2 Ma of the boundary horizon)

#### Integrating multiple stratigraphic tools
- **Biostratigraphy:** first/last appearances of index fossils define biozones; fossils provide global correlation (same fauna = same time); faunal succession is the backbone of relative chronology
- **Chemostratigraphy:** global isotopic events (δ¹³C excursions, δ¹⁸O changes, Sr isotope curve) provide independent correlation signals; many mass extinction boundaries show sharp geochemical anomalies (iridium spike at K-Pg; δ¹³C excursion at the Permian-Triassic boundary)
- **Magnetostratigraphy:** reversals of Earth's magnetic field recorded in volcanic and marine sedimentary rocks; the geomagnetic polarity timescale (GPTS) extends back ~160 Ma with high resolution; oceanic spreading anomalies provide independent age control for the Jurassic–Recent
- **Radiometric dating:** volcanic ash layers (tuffs) interbedded with fossil-bearing sediments are the primary link between relative and absolute time; zircon U-Pb and Ar-Ar are standard; uncertainty in boundary ages is dominated by the stratigraphic distance between the GSSP horizon and the nearest datable ash layer

#### Major Phanerozoic boundaries and their defining events
- Cambrian base (538.8 Ma): base defined by first appearance of trace fossil *Treptichnus pedum*; onset of skeletal metazoans
- Ordovician base (485.4 Ma): base of first graptolite biozone; followed by major marine radiation
- Silurian base (443.8 Ma): end-Ordovician mass extinction (second largest in Phanerozoic); glaciation and sea-level fall
- Devonian base (419.2 Ma): graptolite and conodont zonation; followed by spread of land plants
- Carboniferous base (358.9 Ma): Famennian-Tournasian boundary; end-Devonian extinction
- Permian base (298.9 Ma): defined by conodont *Streptognathodus isolatus*
- Triassic base (251.9 Ma): Permian-Triassic extinction (largest mass extinction; ~90% marine species lost); sharp δ¹³C excursion
- Jurassic base (201.4 Ma): end-Triassic extinction; GSSP at Kuhjoch, Austria; negative δ¹³C excursion
- Cretaceous base (145.0 Ma): Jurassic-Cretaceous boundary poorly constrained; GSSP under review
- Paleogene base / K-Pg boundary (66.0 Ma): Chicxulub impact; iridium anomaly; GSSP at El Kef, Tunisia
- Neogene base (23.0 Ma): Oligocene-Miocene boundary; GSSP at Lemme-Carrosio, Italy
- Quaternary base (2.58 Ma): onset of Northern Hemisphere glaciation; GSSP in Monte San Nicola, Sicily

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading and using the timescale"

#### Using the ICS chart
- The ICS International Chronostratigraphic Chart is published in updated versions (current: v2023/06); freely downloadable
- Colour-coded by commission: Cenozoic warm colours; Mesozoic cool colours; Paleozoic earth tones; Precambrian greys
- Reading ages: each period boundary has a printed age in Ma with ± uncertainty; that uncertainty reflects the quality of radiometric calibration near the GSSP
- Practical use: given a fossil assemblage or lithology, assign it to a system/series; look up the boundary ages; constrain the absolute age to that interval

#### Ongoing revision
- The timescale is a living document: Geologic Time Scale 2020 (GTS2020) updated ~30 boundary ages compared to GTS2012
- Two main sources of revision: (1) new high-precision U-Pb zircon or Ar-Ar dates from volcanic ashes near GSSPs; (2) recognition that a previously used GSSP horizon does not correlate globally as expected, leading to reassignment
- Example of revision: the Cambrian base has been revised multiple times as understanding of *Treptichnus pedum* distribution improved; currently placed at 538.8 Ma (GTS2020) vs. 542.0 Ma (GTS2004)
- "Stratigraphic uncertainty": when a paper reports a fossil from the "Early Jurassic," that means ≤201.4–~182 Ma — a ~20 Ma window, not a single date; combining biostratigraphy (stage identification) with nearby radiometric dates narrows this window

#### Connecting timescale to events
- Concept: the timescale is not arbitrary — most major boundaries mark biological or environmental events that are recognizable worldwide
- The "Big Five" mass extinctions and their boundary ages: end-Ordovician (443.8 Ma), Late Devonian (372 Ma), end-Permian (251.9 Ma), end-Triassic (201.4 Ma), end-Cretaceous (66.0 Ma)
- Each is associated with a distinctive geochemical or paleontological signal that helps define and correlate the boundary globally

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. What is the difference between a "period" and a "system" in the ICS timescale hierarchy, and give an example of each?
2. What is a GSSP and what criteria must a rock section meet to be designated as one?
3. The Permian-Triassic boundary is defined at 251.9 Ma. A geologist finds a rock unit with conodonts typical of the Late Permian below and Early Triassic fossils above. What additional information would best constrain the absolute age of the boundary within that section?
4. Why does the Cretaceous-Paleogene boundary (K-Pg) appear in the rock record as a distinctive geochemical horizon globally, and what event caused this?
5. A researcher reports that their fossil fauna is "Campanian in age." Using the ICS timescale, approximately what range of absolute ages does this imply?
