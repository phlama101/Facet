## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Distinguish oceanic crust from continental crust** by composition (mafic/basaltic vs. felsic/granitic), thickness (~7 km vs. 30–70 km), and density (~3.0 g/cm³ vs. ~2.7 g/cm³).
2. **Explain isostasy** — why less-dense continental crust floats higher on the mantle and oceanic crust sits lower, using the iceberg analogy.
3. **Describe the age contrast** between the two crust types — oceanic crust is never older than ~200 Ma; continental crust preserves rocks billions of years old — and recognize what the age contrast implies about crustal recycling (without yet explaining the plate-tectonic mechanism).
4. **Name at least two methods** geologists use to characterize crustal composition and thickness (seismic surveys, ocean-floor drilling, rock sampling).

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "Two kinds of floor"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** When you walk on a beach, you are standing at the edge of a fundamental boundary — not just between land and water, but between two entirely different types of rock. The ocean floor is not simply flooded land. It is made of a different rock, formed by a different process, and behaves differently under the forces that shape the planet. Lesson 1.1.1 established that the crust ranges from ~7 km to ~70 km thick, but did not explain why. This lesson answers that question.

**What this section establishes.**
- Crust is not uniform — there are two fundamentally different kinds.
- The thickness difference introduced in 1.1.1 is a consequence of composition and density.
- The vocabulary needed for the concept section: oceanic crust, continental crust, basalt, granite.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Oceanic crust** — the thin (~7 km), dense, basaltic crust that forms the ocean floor.
- **Continental crust** — the thick (30–70 km), less dense, granitic crust that forms the continents.
- **Basalt** — a dark, fine-grained volcanic rock that makes up oceanic crust; rich in iron and magnesium.
- **Granite** — a light-colored, coarse-grained rock representative of continental crust; rich in silicon and aluminum.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: Composition, density, isostasy, and age
**Type:** `concept` (with cards for the two crust types and one card for isostasy)
**Estimated reading time:** ~7–8 minutes

**Subsection A — Composition and density.** The single most important difference between the two crust types is chemistry, which drives all the other differences.
- **Oceanic crust** is **mafic**: rich in magnesium and iron. The dominant rock is basalt (and its coarser-grained equivalent, gabbro). Average density ~3.0 g/cm³.
- **Continental crust** is **felsic**: rich in silicon and aluminum. The dominant rock type is granite and its relatives. Average density ~2.7 g/cm³.
- The terms "mafic" and "felsic" will return in Module 1.3 when we classify rocks; for now, the key point is dense vs. less dense.

**Subsection B — Thickness.** Oceanic crust is ~7 km thick — thin and dense. Continental crust averages ~35 km but can reach 70 km beneath young mountain ranges (e.g., the Himalayas). This thickness is directly related to isostasy.

**Subsection C — Isostasy.** This is the central concept of the lesson.
- Imagine two icebergs of different sizes floating in water: the larger iceberg extends both higher above the water and deeper below it. Both float in balance.
- Crustal blocks float on the mantle in the same way. Less dense continental crust extends both higher above sea level AND has a deeper "root" reaching down into the mantle. Dense oceanic crust has a thin root and sits low — which is why the ocean basins exist at all, 3–5 km below sea level.
- **Isostasy** is the term for this gravitational equilibrium. It explains why the two crustal types sit at systematically different elevations without needing to know anything about plate tectonics.

**Subsection D — Age contrast.** Oceanic crust sampled anywhere on the ocean floor is at most ~200 million years old — geologically young. Continental rocks, by contrast, include ancient cratons billions of years old (the oldest dated mineral grain is ~4.4 Ga, from the Jack Hills of Australia). Something must be continuously destroying oceanic crust and replacing it with fresh rock. The mechanism — seafloor spreading and subduction — will be the subject of Module 1.4.

**Evidence methods (brief).** Geologists characterize crustal types using: (1) seismic-wave refraction surveys, which reveal thickness and composition indirectly; (2) ocean-floor drilling (DSDP/ODP/IODP programs), which has recovered basalt cores from across the ocean basins; (3) onshore rock sampling of exposed continental crust. All three lines converge on the same picture.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Composition ID** — which set of properties describes oceanic crust? (thin, dense, basaltic, young — vs. distractors that mix up the two types)
2. **Isostasy** — why does continental crust stand higher above the mantle than oceanic crust? (it is less dense and floats higher, like a larger iceberg — the correct answer; distractors invoke incorrect mechanisms)
3. **Age contrast** — what does the fact that no oceanic crust is older than ~200 Ma imply? (something is continuously recycling it — mechanism to come in Module 1.4)

Each answer choice — correct and distractors — receives a one-sentence explanation within the single `explain` field.

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"If the ocean floor is still 'crust,' why is it so different from the ground beneath our feet?"**
   *Resolved by:* The composition subsection — oceanic crust is basaltic (mafic, dense) while continental crust is granitic (felsic, less dense). Two types of rock, two different origins.

2. **"Why does continental crust sit thousands of meters higher than oceanic crust?"**
   *Resolved by:* The isostasy subsection — less dense crust floats higher on the mantle, exactly as a less dense iceberg sits higher in water.

3. **"The oldest ocean floor is only ~200 million years old, but continents have rocks over 4 billion years old. What does that tell us?"**
   *Resolved by:* The age-contrast subsection — oceanic crust must be continuously recycled, implying an active process that creates and destroys it. The mechanism (seafloor spreading, subduction) is deferred to Module 1.4.
