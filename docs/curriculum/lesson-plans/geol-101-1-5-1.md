## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Reading a volcano's shape"

**Hook:** A geologist who has never visited a volcano can read its eruption history from a photograph of its silhouette. The gentle, domed profile of Mauna Loa — barely 5° slope on its flanks — communicates instantly: basaltic magma, effusive eruptions, hotspot origin. The near-perfect steep cone of Mt. Fuji — 35° slopes, symmetrical, snow-capped — says: andesitic magma, explosive eruptions, subduction arc. The flat, bowl-shaped depression of the Yellowstone caldera — a hole in the landscape rather than a mountain — says: rhyolitic magma, cataclysmic collapse, continental hotspot. A volcano's shape is not arbitrary. It is the accumulated record of every eruption that built or modified it, written in the language of magma composition and eruption dynamics.

**Key Terms:**
- **Shield volcano** — A broad, gently sloping volcanic edifice built by repeated effusive eruptions of low-viscosity basaltic lava. Named for its resemblance to a warrior's shield lying on the ground. Slopes typically 2–10°. Examples: Mauna Loa (Hawaii), Skjaldbreiður (Iceland).
- **Stratovolcano (composite volcano)** — A steep, conical volcanic edifice built by alternating layers of lava flows and pyroclastic material (ash, cinders, bombs). Associated with intermediate to felsic magma and explosive to moderately explosive eruptions. Examples: Mt. Fuji, Mt. Rainier, Mt. St. Helens, Vesuvius.
- **Cinder cone** — A small, steep-sided volcanic cone built from pyroclastic fragments (cinders, scoria) ejected from a single vent. Forms quickly, typically in a single eruptive episode. Examples: Parícutin (Mexico), Sunset Crater (Arizona).
- **Caldera** — A large, roughly circular depression formed by the collapse of a volcanic edifice into a partially emptied magma chamber. Not a crater (which forms by explosion); a caldera forms by subsidence. Examples: Yellowstone, Crater Lake, Campi Flegrei.
- **Lava plateau** — A broad, flat landform built by repeated eruptions of very fluid basaltic lava from fissures rather than a central vent, covering large areas with successive lava flows. Examples: Columbia River Plateau, Deccan Plateau, Iceland's interior.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Five volcanic landforms"

**Body overview:**
- Silica content → viscosity: low-silica (mafic) basalt flows freely; high-silica (felsic) rhyolite is stiff and gas-rich → explosive
- Eruption style spectrum: effusive (lava flows) to explosive (pyroclastic eruptions)
- Five landform types with dimensions, slopes, examples, and controls
- Pyroclastic material: tephra, ash, lapilli, volcanic bombs, cinders/scoria
- Tectonic setting → landform: hotspot oceanic → shield; subduction arc → stratovolcano; continental hotspot → caldera; fissure eruption → lava plateau; monogenetic field → cinder cones
- Reading composite landscapes: many volcanic regions contain multiple landform types

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Mauna Loa (Hawaii) has slopes of ~5° and is one of the largest volcanoes on Earth by volume. Mt. St. Helens (Washington) has slopes of ~30° and is dramatically smaller. What accounts for the difference in slope angle and overall shape?
- A) Mauna Loa is older and has been eroded flat over millions of years; Mt. St. Helens is geologically young and has not yet been significantly eroded
- B) Mauna Loa erupts low-viscosity basaltic lava that flows far from the vent before solidifying, building a broad gentle dome; Mt. St. Helens erupts high-viscosity andesitic to dacitic magma that piles up near the vent and interlayers with pyroclastic deposits, building a steep cone ✓
- C) Mauna Loa sits on a hotspot that supplies magma slowly from directly below, spreading it evenly; Mt. St. Helens receives magma from a subduction zone at an angle, which causes it to build asymmetrically into a steep cone
- D) The difference is eruption frequency: Mauna Loa erupts so often that lava fills its own flanks and smooths the slope; Mt. St. Helens erupts rarely so each eruption adds a discrete steep layer

**Q2:** Crater Lake in Oregon occupies a roughly circular depression 8–10 km across and 600 m deep in the remnant of a much larger volcano. What geological process formed this depression, and why is it called a caldera rather than a crater?
- A) A massive explosive eruption blasted the top off the original Mt. Mazama, excavating the circular depression by explosive force — this is called a caldera because it is larger than a typical summit crater
- B) The magma chamber beneath Mt. Mazama partially emptied during a large eruption ~7,700 years ago, removing support from the overlying volcanic edifice; the summit collapsed inward by subsidence into the evacuated chamber, forming a caldera — a collapse feature, not an explosion hole ✓
- C) Glacial erosion during the last ice age carved a circular bowl into the top of the volcano; the lake formed when the glacier melted; the feature is called a caldera because it occurs on a volcanic summit
- D) The depression is a tectonic graben formed when the crust beneath the volcano was pulled apart by extension; the circular shape reflects the symmetric cooling and contraction of the surrounding volcanic rock

**Q3:** A geologist is studying a region with three distinct volcanic features: (1) a broad, gently sloping dome of basalt; (2) a chain of steep, symmetrical cones with andesitic lava and thick ash deposits; (3) a flat plain of layered basalt extending hundreds of kilometres from a system of ancient fissures. Which tectonic settings most likely produced each feature?
- A) (1) subduction arc, (2) oceanic hotspot, (3) continental rift
- B) (1) oceanic hotspot, (2) subduction volcanic arc, (3) flood basalt from a mantle plume head or fissure system ✓
- C) (1) continental hotspot, (2) transform boundary, (3) divergent spreading centre
- D) (1) divergent boundary, (2) continental hotspot, (3) subduction arc back-basin
