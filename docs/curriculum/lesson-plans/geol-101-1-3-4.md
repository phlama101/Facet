## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Rock transformed"

**Hook:** A piece of Carrara marble — smooth, white, translucent at the edges — was once ordinary limestone on an ancient seafloor, built from the compressed shells of marine organisms. When the African plate began closing the ancient Tethys Ocean and colliding with Europe, the seafloor limestone was buried, heated, and squeezed. The calcite grains recrystallised into interlocking crystals without ever melting, erasing the original shell textures and producing a uniform, sparkling rock. Michelangelo's David began as a seashell. Metamorphic rocks are the most dramatic evidence that rocks are not permanent — they are constantly being remade by Earth's internal processes.

**Key Terms:**
- **Metamorphism** — The transformation of pre-existing rock (of any family) by elevated temperature, pressure, or both, without fully melting the rock. Minerals recrystallise in the solid state into assemblages stable under the new conditions.
- **Protolith** — The original rock from which a metamorphic rock formed. Marble's protolith is limestone; quartzite's protolith is sandstone; schist's protolith is typically shale or mudstone.
- **Foliation** — A planar fabric in metamorphic rock produced by the parallel alignment of flat or elongated minerals (micas, amphiboles) under directed pressure. Foliation planes are perpendicular to the direction of maximum compressive stress.
- **Metamorphic grade** — The intensity of metamorphism experienced by a rock, reflecting the maximum temperature and pressure conditions reached. Low-grade rocks (slate, phyllite) formed at lower temperatures; high-grade rocks (schist, gneiss) formed at higher temperatures.
- **Contact metamorphism** — Metamorphism driven primarily by heat from a nearby igneous intrusion, without significant directed pressure. Produces a halo of altered rock (aureole) around the intrusion; typically non-foliated.
- **Regional metamorphism** — Metamorphism driven by both elevated temperature and directed pressure across a large region, typically in a mountain belt root zone. Produces foliated rocks over areas of thousands of square kilometres.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Pressure, heat, transformation"

**Body overview:**
- Solid-state recrystallisation: atoms migrate and rebond into new mineral assemblages without melting
- Two metamorphic settings: contact (heat only, aureole around intrusion, non-foliated) and regional (heat + directed pressure, mountain belts, foliated)
- Foliation: micas and amphiboles grow with their flat faces perpendicular to compressive stress → planar fabric
- Grade sequence from shale protolith: shale → slate (low) → phyllite → schist (medium) → gneiss (high)
- Non-foliated: marble (limestone protolith, equidimensional calcite, no preferred orientation) and quartzite (sandstone protolith, fused quartz grains)
- Index minerals: diagnostic minerals that appear at specific P-T conditions, used to map metamorphic zones

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** What is the key distinction between metamorphism and igneous rock formation?
- A) Metamorphism only occurs at Earth's surface; igneous rocks form underground
- B) Metamorphism transforms rock in the solid state through heat and/or pressure without melting; igneous rock forms by cooling of a melt  ✓
- C) Metamorphism always requires water; igneous rocks form from dry magma
- D) There is no fundamental distinction — both involve mineral recrystallisation from high temperatures

**Q2:** Shale metamorphosed to schist develops a strong foliation, but limestone metamorphosed to marble has no foliation. Why?
- A) Marble forms at higher temperatures that destroy foliation after it develops; schist forms at lower temperatures that preserve it
- B) Shale contains flat clay minerals and micas that align under directed pressure, creating foliation; limestone is composed of equidimensional calcite grains that recrystallise without preferred orientation, producing no foliation  ✓
- C) Limestone is too soft to develop foliation — only hard rocks can develop the planar fabric
- D) Marble forms by contact metamorphism near intrusions while schist always forms by regional metamorphism; the rock type determines the metamorphic type

**Q3:** A geologist finds a zone of altered, fine-grained non-foliated rock directly surrounding a granite pluton, grading back to unaltered country rock over a distance of 200 metres. What type of metamorphism does this describe, and what is the zone of altered rock called?
- A) Regional metamorphism — the altered zone is called a metamorphic belt, produced by tectonic compression associated with the intrusion
- B) Contact metamorphism — the heat from the cooling granite pluton baked the surrounding rock in a thermal aureole without significant directed pressure, producing non-foliated hornfels  ✓
- C) Contact metamorphism — but the altered zone is called a subduction zone because the granite intruded along a plate boundary
- D) Dynamic metamorphism — frictional heat from fault movement along the granite contact produced the altered zone
