## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Describe the two-part core structure** — solid inner core and liquid outer core — and briefly connect the outer core's liquidity to the S-wave evidence established in Lesson 1.1.1 (referenced but not re-taught).
2. **Explain the geodynamo** — how convection of electrically conducting liquid iron in the outer core, organized by Earth's rotation, generates and sustains the global magnetic field.
3. **Describe the magnetosphere** and its function as a shield against the solar wind, explaining why this protection is critical for maintaining an atmosphere and allowing life.
4. **Explain magnetic reversals** — what they are, that they have occurred hundreds of times in geologic history, and how magnetic striping on the ocean floor provides evidence for both reversals and seafloor spreading (tying together the threads from Lessons 1.1.2 and 1.1.3).

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "The invisible shield"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Right now, a stream of high-energy charged particles is hurtling toward Earth from the Sun at roughly 400–800 km per second — the solar wind. Without protection, it would gradually strip away Earth's atmosphere, destroy the ozone layer, and bombard the surface with radiation. We are alive, in part, because an invisible magnetic field generated deep inside the planet deflects this stream before it reaches us. That field originates thousands of kilometres underground, in the churning liquid iron of Earth's outer core.

**What this section establishes.**
- The solar wind is a real and continuous threat; the magnetic field is the response.
- The connection back to 1.1.1: the outer core is liquid iron-nickel (established there); this lesson explains what that liquid does beyond sitting at depth.
- The connection back to 1.1.3: the core is the heat source for mantle convection; the core also convects internally.
- Vocabulary needed for the concept section.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Geodynamo** — the self-sustaining process by which convection of liquid iron in the outer core generates Earth's magnetic field; powered by the same heat that drives mantle convection.
- **Magnetosphere** — the region of space around Earth dominated and shaped by Earth's magnetic field; it deflects most of the solar wind and extends tens of thousands of kilometres into space.
- **Solar wind** — a continuous stream of charged particles (mainly protons and electrons) emitted by the Sun; without a planetary magnetic field, it erodes planetary atmospheres over geological time.
- **Magnetic reversal** — an event in which Earth's north and south magnetic poles switch positions; reversals have occurred hundreds of times in the past and are recorded in the magnetic properties of rocks.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The dynamo, the shield, and the record in rock
**Type:** `concept` (with three cards)
**Estimated reading time:** ~8–9 minutes

**Subsection A — The core, briefly revisited.** Lesson 1.1.1 established the two-part core: the inner core is solid iron-nickel (~1,200 km radius); the outer core is liquid iron-nickel (~2,200 km thick). Both are at extreme temperatures — roughly 4,000–6,000 °C. The outer core's liquidity (proved by the S-wave shadow zone) is exactly what makes the geodynamo possible: a solid inner core cannot convect or carry electric currents in the relevant way.

**Subsection B — The geodynamo.** Three ingredients combine to produce Earth's magnetic field:
1. **A conducting fluid**: liquid iron is an excellent conductor of electricity. Unlike solid rock, it can carry large electrical currents.
2. **Convection**: the outer core convects for the same reason the mantle does — it is hotter at the bottom (heated by the inner core and by radioactive decay) than at the top. Hot liquid iron rises, cools slightly, and sinks — a circulation pattern very similar to mantle convection but faster and in liquid rather than solid rock.
3. **Rotation (the Coriolis effect)**: Earth's rotation deflects moving fluids — this is the same effect that makes hurricanes spiral. In the outer core, the Coriolis effect organises the convecting iron into large helical columns aligned with Earth's rotation axis. These rotating electrical conductors generate electric currents; those currents generate a magnetic field; the magnetic field, in turn, influences the flow of the conducting fluid in a way that sustains the currents — a self-reinforcing feedback loop. This is the geodynamo.

The result is a global magnetic field that, at Earth's surface, resembles the field of a bar magnet aligned roughly with the rotation axis — with a north magnetic pole near the geographic north pole and a south magnetic pole near the geographic south pole. (The magnetic poles are not exactly at the geographic poles, and they wander slowly over time.)

**Subsection C — The magnetosphere.** Earth's magnetic field does not stop at the surface. It extends far into space, creating a protective bubble called the **magnetosphere**. On the sunward side, the solar wind compresses it to about 10 Earth radii. On the night side, the wind stretches it into a long tail hundreds of Earth radii long.

The solar wind cannot easily penetrate the magnetosphere. Most of the charged particles are deflected around Earth rather than reaching the surface. This deflection has two important consequences: (1) it protects the atmosphere from being eroded over geological time — Earth has had liquid water and a substantial atmosphere for over 4 billion years in part because the magnetic field has been present for most of that time; (2) where the field funnels particles down toward the poles, the collisions with atmospheric molecules produce the **aurora** (Northern and Southern Lights) — a visible signature of the magnetosphere at work.

The comparison with Mars is instructive: Mars lost its global magnetic field roughly 4 billion years ago, probably because its smaller iron core cooled and solidified. Without that shield, the solar wind has steadily stripped Mars's atmosphere, leaving it a thin sliver of CO₂ at less than 1% of Earth's surface pressure.

**Subsection D — Magnetic reversals.** Earth's magnetic field is not permanent — it has reversed its polarity hundreds of times over geologic history. In a reversal, north and south magnetic poles gradually swap positions. Reversals are irregular in timing: some periods see a reversal every few hundred thousand years; others, like the "Cretaceous Normal Superchron," went 40 million years without one. The last reversal — the Brunhes-Matuyama boundary — occurred about 780,000 years ago.

How do we know? The evidence is locked into the ocean floor. As new oceanic crust solidifies at mid-ocean ridges (as introduced in Lessons 1.1.2 and 1.1.3), tiny iron-bearing minerals in the cooling basalt align with Earth's magnetic field at the moment of solidification — like compass needles frozen in rock. When the field later reverses, the next strip of new crust records the reversed polarity. The result is parallel bands of normally and reversely magnetised rock on either side of every mid-ocean ridge, perfectly symmetric about the ridge axis. This **magnetic striping** was one of the key pieces of evidence that convinced geologists in the 1960s that seafloor spreading was real — and it neatly ties together the core, mantle convection, and crustal creation in a single observable record.

During a reversal, the field weakens but does not switch off instantly. Evidence from sediment and rock records suggests the transition takes thousands to tens of thousands of years, during which the field is weaker and more complex in shape — but not absent.

**Subsection E — Module 1.1 closing.** With this lesson, Module 1.1 is complete. We have moved from the surface to the center: the crust (Lessons 1.1.1, 1.1.2), the mantle and its convection (1.1.3), and now the core and its dynamo (1.1.4). Every layer has been introduced with its composition, physical state, and the evidence that reveals it. Module 1.2 zooms back to the surface to ask: what are Earth's materials actually made of, at the mineral level?

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Geodynamo mechanism** — which combination of factors generates Earth's magnetic field? (liquid iron + convection + Earth's rotation — the three ingredients; distractors isolate only one factor or invoke incorrect mechanisms)
2. **Magnetosphere function** — what would most likely happen to Earth's atmosphere over geological time without a magnetic field? (gradual erosion by the solar wind, as occurred on Mars; distractors confuse the magnetosphere with the ozone layer or gravity)
3. **Magnetic striping evidence** — what does the symmetric pattern of normally and reversely magnetised rock on either side of mid-ocean ridges demonstrate? (both magnetic reversals and seafloor spreading; distractors attribute it to a single cause or misidentify what the pattern records)

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"The outer core is liquid iron — so what? Why does that matter for life on the surface?"**
   *Resolved by:* The geodynamo and magnetosphere subsections — liquid iron convecting in a rotating planet generates a magnetic field; that field deflects the solar wind; without it, Earth's atmosphere erodes (as Mars demonstrates).

2. **"How can we know Earth's magnetic field has flipped — no human being has witnessed a complete reversal?"**
   *Resolved by:* The magnetic reversal and magnetic striping subsections — iron minerals in solidifying basalt at mid-ocean ridges record the field direction at the moment of cooling, leaving a permanent rock record spanning hundreds of millions of years.

3. **"How does the core connect to everything else we've learned in Module 1.1?"**
   *Resolved by:* The module-closing paragraph — the core heats the mantle (1.1.3), whose convection creates and destroys the oceanic crust (1.1.2), whose properties were first identified through seismology that also revealed the core's structure (1.1.1). The magnetic striping ties ocean-floor creation directly to the core's magnetic-reversal record.
