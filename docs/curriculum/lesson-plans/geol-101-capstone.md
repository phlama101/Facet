## 25 Questions — Outline

### Module 1.1 — Earth's Internal Structure (Q1–5)

**Q1 — Core states / seismic shadow zone**  
Why do P-waves slow and refract but S-waves disappear entirely beyond ~105° from an earthquake — and what does this tell us about the outer core?  
Correct: S-waves cannot travel through liquid; the outer core is molten iron-nickel; P-waves refract through the liquid but at lower velocity, creating the shadow zone.

**Q2 — Crust types: composition and thickness**  
What are the two types of crust, how do they differ in composition and density, and why does oceanic crust subduct while continental crust does not?  
Correct: oceanic = basaltic (~3.0 g/cm³, thin ~7 km); continental = granitic (~2.7 g/cm³, thick ~35 km); oceanic is denser → sinks beneath lighter continental crust at convergent margins.

**Q3 — Mantle convection**  
What drives convection in the mantle and how does it relate to plate motion?  
Correct: heat from core + radioactive decay creates temperature differences → hotter mantle rises, cooler sinks → slow convective flow; plates move partly by being dragged by mantle flow and partly by slab pull at subduction zones.

**Q4 — Core and magnetic field**  
What generates Earth's magnetic field and why is a liquid outer core essential to the process?  
Correct: convection in the liquid iron-nickel outer core generates electric currents via dynamo effect → produces the geomagnetic field; without liquid flow, no dynamo → no field (Mars lost its field when its outer core solidified).

**Q5 — Moho and internal layering**  
What is the Mohorovičić discontinuity, how was it discovered, and what property of seismic waves reveals it?  
Correct: seismic velocity increase at the crust-mantle boundary (P-waves speed up ~6 km/s in crust → ~8 km/s in mantle); Mohorovičić noticed arrival-time anomalies in 1909 Croatian earthquake data; refracted waves through denser mantle arrive before direct crustal waves beyond ~200 km.

### Module 1.2 — Minerals (Q6–10)

**Q6 — Mineral definition**  
Which combination of properties must ALL minerals satisfy?  
Correct: naturally occurring, inorganic, solid, with a crystalline atomic structure and a definite (or range of) chemical composition. Coal is organic → not a mineral. Glass is amorphous → not a mineral.

**Q7 — Identification: streak vs colour**  
Why is streak more reliable than surface colour for mineral identification?  
Correct: surface colour is affected by impurities, oxidation, weathering; streak (the colour of the powder) reflects the mineral's intrinsic composition; iron minerals vary in colour but have consistent streaks (hematite: red-brown regardless of surface lustre).

**Q8 — Silicates as dominant group**  
Why are silicate minerals the most abundant group in Earth's crust, and which two silicates are most common?  
Correct: Si and O are the two most abundant elements in the crust; silicate tetrahedra (SiO₄) polymerise into chains, sheets, and frameworks; feldspars (~41% of crust) and quartz (~12%) are most abundant; together with micas, amphiboles, and pyroxenes they make up ~90% of crust.

**Q9 — Crystal size and cooling rate**  
A geologist finds two igneous rocks with identical mineral composition. One has centimetre-scale crystals; the other is fine-grained. What does this reveal about their cooling histories?  
Correct: grain size reflects cooling rate; coarse crystals = slow cooling (magma cooled deep underground → plutonic/intrusive); fine-grained = rapid cooling (lava erupted at surface → volcanic/extrusive); same composition means same original magma.

**Q10 — Cleavage vs fracture**  
What causes minerals to cleave along flat planes rather than fracture irregularly?  
Correct: cleavage follows planes of weak atomic bonding in the crystal lattice; mica cleaves perfectly in one direction (weak interlayer bonds); calcite cleaves in three directions (rhombohedral); quartz has no cleavage (strong 3D SiO₄ framework in all directions) and fractures conchoidally.

### Module 1.3 — The Three Rock Families (Q11–15)

**Q11 — Rock cycle integration**  
A granite bathólith is uplifted, exposed by erosion, weathered to sediment, buried and lithified, then subducted and partially melted. Trace this path through the rock cycle and name each rock type produced.  
Correct: granite (igneous) → erosion → sediment → compaction/cementation → sandstone or conglomerate (sedimentary) → subduction heat/pressure → metasandstone/quartzite (metamorphic) → partial melt → new igneous rock; the rock cycle is continuous, not directional.

**Q12 — Igneous classification**  
What two variables are used to classify igneous rocks, and what do basalt and granite represent at opposite ends of those variables?  
Correct: texture (grain size = cooling rate: intrusive coarse, extrusive fine) and composition (SiO₂ content: mafic/basaltic ~50% SiO₂ vs felsic/granitic ~70% SiO₂); basalt = fine-grained, mafic (low silica, high Fe/Mg); granite = coarse-grained, felsic (high silica, K/Na feldspar + quartz).

**Q13 — Sedimentary rock types**  
What distinguishes clastic, chemical, and biogenic (organic) sedimentary rocks, with one example each?  
Correct: clastic = fragments of pre-existing rock cemented together (sandstone, shale, conglomerate); chemical = precipitated directly from solution (rock salt/halite, chert, travertine); biogenic = accumulated organic material or skeletal matter (coal, limestone/chalk from shells, diatomite).

**Q14 — Metamorphic grade and index minerals**  
Why are garnet, staurolite, and kyanite called index minerals, and what does finding them in a rock tell a geologist?  
Correct: each index mineral is stable only within a specific range of temperature and pressure; finding a mineral tells you the minimum temperature/pressure conditions the rock reached during metamorphism; garnet (moderate T/P) → staurolite (higher) → kyanite/sillimanite (high) allows mapping of metamorphic grade across a region.

**Q15 — Foliation in metamorphic rocks**  
Why do schists and gneisses develop foliation (layered fabric), while marble and quartzite do not?  
Correct: foliation develops when platy or elongate minerals (micas, amphiboles) grow perpendicular to directed pressure during regional metamorphism → preferred alignment → visible layering; marble (recrystallised calcite) and quartzite (recrystallised quartz) are made of equidimensional crystals that do not align into foliation under pressure.

### Module 1.4 — Plate Tectonics (Q16–20)

**Q16 — Evidence for plate tectonics**  
Name three independent lines of evidence that support the theory of plate tectonics and explain what each demonstrates.  
Correct: (1) paleomagnetism — magnetic stripes symmetric about mid-ocean ridges record reversals and seafloor spreading; (2) seafloor age — ocean crust oldest at margins, youngest at ridges, confirming seafloor spreading; (3) fit of continents + matching fossil distributions and geological belts across the Atlantic.

**Q17 — Boundary types and features**  
What geologic features are associated with each of the three types of plate boundary?  
Correct: divergent → rift valleys, mid-ocean ridges, basaltic volcanism, normal faults, new ocean crust; convergent → subduction zones (oceanic-continental → volcanic arc + trench; continental-continental → mountain belt, no subduction); transform → strike-slip faults, earthquakes, no volcanism, no new crust (San Andreas, Dead Sea Transform).

**Q18 — Subduction and volcanic arcs**  
Why does subduction produce andesitic (intermediate composition) volcanoes in a volcanic arc rather than the basaltic volcanoes of mid-ocean ridges?  
Correct: subducting oceanic crust releases water as it dehydrates; water lowers the melting point of the overlying mantle wedge (flux melting) → basaltic melt rises; as it passes through and assimilates continental or arc crust, it evolves toward intermediate (andesitic) composition; the arc therefore erupts more silicic, more explosive magma than MOR basalts.

**Q19 — Orogeny: Himalayas vs Andes**  
Compare the formation of the Himalayas with the Andes. What type of plate collision formed each, and why is one a volcanic arc and the other not?  
Correct: Andes = oceanic-continental convergence (Nazca Plate subducting under South America) → active volcanic arc, deep trench offshore; Himalayas = continental-continental collision (India into Eurasia, no subduction possible because continental crust is too buoyant to sink) → no volcanism, but extreme crustal thickening and the highest mountain belt on Earth.

**Q20 — Hotspot chains**  
The Hawaiian Islands form a chain where age increases systematically to the northwest. What does this tell us about the direction and speed of the Pacific Plate?  
Correct: the Hawaiian hotspot is a fixed (or near-fixed) plume in the mantle; the Pacific Plate moves over it; older islands to the NW record earlier positions of the plate over the plume; age progression allows calculation of plate velocity (~7–9 cm/yr); the bend in the Emperor Seamount chain records a change in Pacific Plate motion direction ~47 Ma ago.

### Module 1.5 — Reading the Landscape (Q21–25)

**Q21 — Shield vs composite volcanoes**  
Why does Mauna Loa (Hawaii) have gentle slopes and effusive eruptions while Mount St. Helens (Cascades) has steep slopes and explosive eruptions? What controls the difference?  
Correct: magma composition and viscosity; Mauna Loa erupts low-silica basaltic magma (low viscosity) → gas escapes easily → effusive lava flows → broad shield shape; Mt. St. Helens erupts high-silica andesitic/dacitic magma (high viscosity) → gas cannot escape → pressure builds → explosive eruptions → pyroclastic deposits and steep stratovolcano cone.

**Q22 — Weathering controls**  
A limestone plateau in the humid tropics has caves, sinkholes, and limestone towers. A granite outcrop in the Arctic has frost-shattered boulders and angular talus. What controls the dominant weathering type in each setting?  
Correct: climate + rock type; humid tropics → abundant liquid water + warm temperatures → chemical weathering dominates → dissolution of limestone in carbonic acid → karst; Arctic → freeze-thaw cycles → physical weathering dominates → frost wedging fractures granite; limestone dissolves readily (chemical), granite resists dissolution but fractures mechanically.

**Q23 — River systems and fluvial landforms**  
A river in its upper reach has a steep gradient and cuts a V-shaped valley. 400 km downstream, it meanders across a broad floodplain. Explain the gradient control on this transition and identify one erosional and one depositional landform in each reach.  
Correct: steep gradient → high velocity → high erosive energy → V-valley (downcutting) + waterfalls (resistant caprock); gentle gradient → low velocity → energy insufficient to transport full load → deposition; lower reach: meanders (point bars deposit on inside, cut banks erode outside) + oxbow lakes (meander cutoffs); the transition from erosion to deposition is driven entirely by gradient reduction.

**Q24 — Base level**  
A dam is built on a river. Describe what happens to the river system immediately upstream and downstream of the dam over the following decades, using the concept of base level.  
Correct: dam creates new higher local base level upstream → river loses gradient → deposits sediment in reservoir (builds a delta at reservoir head); downstream: dam traps all sediment → clear water released has excess erosive energy → scours downstream channel (incision below dam); delta and downstream floodplain are starved of sediment and erode; the entire system adjusts to the new base-level conditions.

**Q25 — Glacial + coastal integration**  
During a glacial maximum, sea level drops 120 m. When the ice melts, sea level rises 120 m. Describe one landform that records the glacial period on land, one coastal landform that records the sea-level rise, and explain the hydrological mechanism linking ice volume to sea level.  
Correct: land: terminal moraine (marks farthest ice advance) / U-shaped valley / cirque / drumlin field; coastal: fjord (glacial trough flooded by rising sea) / ria / drowned river estuary / new barrier islands built on the freshly flooded shelf; mechanism: water evaporated from ocean accumulates as ice (doesn't return to ocean) → sea level drops; ice melts → water returns → sea level rises; this is glacio-eustasy — the direct coupling of ice volume and ocean volume.
