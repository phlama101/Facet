## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Packages of time"
**Hook:** In the 1970s, a group of Exxon geoscientists — Peter Vail, Robert Mitchum, and colleagues — were analysing seismic reflection profiles from continental margins around the world. They noticed something remarkable: rocks did not simply pile up layer by layer. Instead, they formed discrete packages, each package bounded above and below by surfaces that could be traced continuously across hundreds of kilometres of seismic data. These bounding surfaces were unconformities — or surfaces conformable with them in the basin centre. The packages between them recorded a coherent history of sea-level rise and fall. Vail and Mitchum called these packages depositional sequences, and the discipline they founded — sequence stratigraphy — became one of the most powerful tools in sedimentary geology. It allowed geologists to correlate rock packages across entire basins using a common framework, to predict where reservoir sands and source shales would occur before drilling, and ultimately to reconstruct the history of sea-level change with global resolution.

**Key terms:** depositional sequence, sequence boundary, systems tract, lowstand, transgressive, highstand, falling-stage, maximum flooding surface, transgressive surface, accommodation, Wheeler diagram.

### Section 2 — Concept: "How sequences form"

#### The sequence concept
- A **depositional sequence** is a relatively conformable succession of genetically related strata bounded above and below by unconformities (at the basin margin) or their correlative conformities (in the basin centre where deposition was continuous)
- The bounding surfaces are **sequence boundaries (SB)**: they form when relative sea level falls, causing subaerial exposure and erosion on the shelf while the basin centre may continue to receive sediment
- One sequence = one complete cycle of relative sea-level change (fall → lowstand → rise → highstand → renewed fall)
- The concept unifies surface geology (where unconformities are visible) and subsurface geology (where the correlative conformity is the equivalent horizon in the basin)

#### Controls: the accommodation equation
- **Relative sea level** = eustasy (global sea level) + subsidence (or − uplift); it controls how much space exists for sediment to accumulate
- **Accommodation** = space available for sediment; created by subsidence + eustatic rise; destroyed by eustatic fall + uplift
- **Sediment supply (S)** = rate at which sediment fills accommodation
- Three outcomes: (1) A > S → transgression (water deepens); (2) A = S → shoreline stable; (3) A < S → regression (forced or normal)
- **Normal regression:** accommodation still positive but sediment supply > accommodation creation → shoreline progrades even as sea level rises slightly
- **Forced regression:** accommodation destroyed because sea level is falling faster than sediment can keep up → shoreline forced to step down basinward regardless of sediment supply

#### The four systems tracts
Each depositional sequence is subdivided into systems tracts — packages of coeval depositional systems linked by their position in the sea-level cycle:

- **Falling-Stage Systems Tract (FSST):** deposited during the falling limb of the sea-level curve; forced regression; shelf is exposed and eroded; sand delivered directly to the shelf edge and slope; basin-floor fans may form; bounded below by the previous maximum flooding surface and above by the sequence boundary
- **Lowstand Systems Tract (LST):** deposited when sea level is at or near its minimum; rivers incise valleys on the exposed shelf; sediment bypasses the shelf and accumulates as lowstand fans and slope aprons in deep water; shelf-margin deltas may prograde; bounded below by the sequence boundary
- **Transgressive Systems Tract (TST):** deposited during sea-level rise; shoreline steps landward (retrogrades); sediment is trapped in estuaries and shorefaces landward of the shelf edge; deepening-upward; bounded below by the transgressive surface (TS, also called the first flooding surface) and above by the maximum flooding surface (MFS)
- **Highstand Systems Tract (HST):** deposited when sea level is high but decelerating; accommodation creation slows; sediment supply begins to outpace accommodation → normal regression; shoreline progrades basinward; coarsening-upward shallowing packages; bounded below by the maximum flooding surface and above by the sequence boundary

#### Key surfaces
- **Sequence Boundary (SB):** unconformity on the shelf/platform; correlative conformity in the basin; marks the base of the FSST or LST and top of the HST of the underlying sequence
- **Transgressive Surface (TS):** the first flooding surface after the lowstand; marks the base of the TST; commonly a sharp ravinement surface (wave erosion during transgression) overlain by a thin transgressive lag
- **Maximum Flooding Surface (MFS):** the deepest, most landward position of the shoreline; maximum water depth; finest-grained, most distal facies; often organic-rich shale (source rock); marks the boundary between TST below and HST above; often identified by maximum gamma-ray (shale content) in wireline logs

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading sequences in outcrop and subsurface"

#### The Wheeler diagram
- A conventional stratigraphic cross-section shows thickness and lateral facies change but compresses time — it does not show where time is missing
- A **Wheeler diagram** (chronostratigraphic chart) plots the same data with geological time on the vertical axis and geographic position on the horizontal axis; conformable deposits appear as filled areas; unconformities appear as gaps (white space) where time is missing
- Wheeler diagrams make it immediately clear: (1) where and when the sequence boundaries occur; (2) how long each hiatus lasted and where it was longest (basin margin) vs. shortest (basin centre); (3) the correlation between coeval deposits across the basin
- The maximum flooding surface appears as a horizontal line that persists across the entire basin — it is the one surface that is truly time-equivalent everywhere (because it records a global moment of maximum flooding)

#### Practical applications
- **Petroleum exploration:** sequence stratigraphy predicts where reservoir sands accumulate (LST fans, HST prograding wedges), where source rocks occur (MFS organic-rich shales), and where traps form (structural closures against sequence boundaries, stratigraphic pinch-outs of LST fans)
- **Correlation:** sequences and their bounding surfaces can be traced across entire sedimentary basins and between basins on different continents, providing a global correlation framework independent of biostratigraphy
- **Sea-level reconstruction:** the stacking pattern of sequences encodes the history of relative sea-level change; comparing multiple basins allows global (eustatic) sea-level curves to be reconstructed
