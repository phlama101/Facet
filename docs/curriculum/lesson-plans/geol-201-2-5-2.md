## Learning Objectives

1. Distinguish brittle from ductile deformation and explain the conditions (depth, temperature, strain rate) that control which type occurs.
2. Identify and describe the geometric elements of a fold — hinge, limbs, axial plane, plunge — and distinguish anticlines from synclines.
3. Classify faults by kinematics (normal, reverse/thrust, strike-slip) and explain the tectonic setting associated with each.
4. Describe the field evidence used to recognise faults in outcrop — slickenlines, fault breccia, drag folding, offset markers.
5. Define horst and graben and explain their relationship to extensional tectonics.

## Scope Boundaries

**Covers:** Stress and strain; brittle vs. ductile deformation; fold anatomy and classification (anticline, syncline, overturned, recumbent); fault kinematics (normal, reverse, thrust, strike-slip); field recognition of faults; horst and graben; common tectonic settings for each structure type.
**Does not cover:** Stereonet analysis, quantitative stress tensor, balancing cross-sections, detailed metamorphic grade, seismic reflection interpretation.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The rock record of tectonic force"
**Hook:** The Himalayan mountain belt is the product of two continents colliding at roughly 5 cm per year for the past 50 million years. The result — visible from space — is 2,500 km of folded and thrust-faulted rock, piled up in sheets kilometres thick. At the other extreme, the Basin and Range province of the western United States is being pulled apart: hundreds of normal fault-bounded basins and uplifted ranges stretching across Nevada, Utah, and Arizona. Every fold, every fault, every slickenlined surface in the field is a record of a force applied to rock at a particular depth, temperature, and rate. Understanding that record — reading the structures to infer the forces — is the core of structural geology.

**Key terms:** stress, strain, brittle deformation, ductile deformation, fold, fault, anticline, syncline, axial plane, normal fault, reverse fault, thrust fault, strike-slip fault, horst, graben, slickenlines.

### Section 2 — Concept: "How rocks deform"

#### Stress and strain — brittle vs. ductile
- **Stress**: force per unit area applied to a rock body. Three principal types relevant to geology: *compressional stress* (squeezing — σ₁ > σ₃, layers shorten and thicken), *tensional stress* (extension — σ₃ < σ₁, layers lengthen and thin), *shear stress* (lateral displacement along a plane).
- **Strain**: the resulting deformation — shortening, extension, or shear. Elastic strain recovers when stress is removed (seismic waves); plastic/permanent strain does not.
- **Brittle deformation**: rock fractures rather than flows. Occurs at shallow depths (low confining pressure), low temperatures, and/or high strain rates. Results in faults and joints.
- **Ductile deformation**: rock flows without fracturing, like very stiff putty. Occurs at depth (high confining pressure), high temperatures, and/or slow strain rates. Results in folds and foliation.
- The transition from brittle to ductile is not sharp but occurs at roughly the depth where temperature exceeds ~300°C for quartz-rich crust (~15–20 km in typical continental crust). The same rock that fractures at the surface can fold at depth.

#### Fold anatomy and types
- **Fold elements**: *hinge line* (line of maximum curvature — the crest of a fold); *limbs* (the flanking dipping panels); *axial plane* (the plane containing all hinge lines of a fold — divides it symmetrically); *plunge* (the angle at which the hinge line departs from horizontal; a plunging anticline has a nose that dips in the plunge direction).
- **Anticline**: beds arch upward (convex up); oldest rocks exposed in the core (hinge zone); limbs dip away from the axial plane.
- **Syncline**: beds sag downward (concave up); youngest rocks in the core; limbs dip toward the axial plane.
- **Overturned fold**: one limb has been tilted past vertical so both limbs dip in the same direction; one limb youngs downward (inverted stratigraphy).
- **Recumbent fold**: the axial plane is nearly horizontal; both limbs are sub-horizontal; extreme ductile deformation, common in high-grade metamorphic terranes and deep fold belts.
- **Isocline**: limbs are parallel; tightly appressed fold; high strain.

### Section 3 — Application: "Faults and how to recognise them"

#### Fault classification
- **Normal fault**: the hanging wall (the block above the fault plane) moves *down* relative to the footwall. Caused by tensional stress (extension). The fault plane typically dips at 55–70°. Common in rift zones (East African Rift, Basin and Range, mid-ocean ridges), passive margins. Produces **graben** (downthrown block) and **horst** (upthrown block) topography.
- **Reverse fault**: the hanging wall moves *up* relative to the footwall. Caused by compressional stress (shortening). Fault plane typically dips at >45°. Common in convergent margins and orogenic belts.
- **Thrust fault**: a low-angle reverse fault (dip <45°, often <30°). The hanging wall is pushed over the footwall over long distances (tens to hundreds of km). Characteristic of fold-and-thrust belts (Rockies, Alps, Himalayas, Appalachians). **Décollements** (detachment surfaces) are sub-horizontal thrust faults.
- **Strike-slip fault**: horizontal motion, with the fault plane approximately vertical. **Dextral** (right-lateral): the block across the fault moves to the right as you face the fault. **Sinistral** (left-lateral): moves to the left. Common at transform plate boundaries (San Andreas, North Anatolian) and as lateral ramps in fold-and-thrust belts.
- **Oblique-slip faults**: most real faults have a component of both dip-slip and strike-slip motion.

#### Field recognition of faults
- **Slickenlines (slickensides)**: linear striations or ridges on the fault plane polished by friction during slip. Orientation records the direction of last movement; asymmetry of steps indicates sense of motion (steps face in the direction the opposite block moved).
- **Fault breccia**: angular, broken rock fragments along the fault plane produced by brittle grinding during slip. At greater depth, fine-grained **mylonite** forms by ductile grinding.
- **Drag folding**: competent beds bent near a fault surface as they were dragged during fault motion; the fold geometry indicates the sense of motion.
- **Offset markers**: the most definitive evidence — a previously continuous feature (dyke, bed, stream, road) that has been offset. The direction and magnitude of offset define the fault kinematics.

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. Under what depth, temperature, and strain-rate conditions does rock deform in a brittle manner versus a ductile manner? Give an example of a geological structure produced by each mode of deformation.
2. Describe the geometric difference between an anticline and a syncline. If you are standing in the field looking at a series of tilted sedimentary beds, what single observation most quickly tells you whether you are on the limb of an anticline or a syncline?
3. You find a fault in the field. The fault plane dips at 60°. The rocks above the fault (hanging wall) are stratigraphically *older* than the rocks below (footwall). What type of fault is this? What tectonic environment is it consistent with?
4. Describe three independent lines of field evidence that you could use to confirm that a contact between two rock units is a fault rather than a normal stratigraphic contact.
5. On a geological map of a continental rift zone, you observe a series of tilted fault blocks — alternating uplifted ranges and down-dropped basins. What type of faults bound these blocks? What is the terminology for the uplifted blocks and the down-dropped basins? Explain the stress regime that produced this landscape.
