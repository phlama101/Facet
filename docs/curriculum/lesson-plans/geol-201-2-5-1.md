## Learning Objectives

1. Interpret contour lines on a topographic map, including identifying ridge and valley forms and estimating slope steepness.
2. Define strike and dip, explain how each is measured, and read a strike-and-dip symbol on a geological map.
3. Interpret the standard symbols used on geological maps — formation colours, contact types, and structural indicators.
4. Explain how a geological cross-section is constructed from map data, and apply the V-rule to predict how layered rocks intersect topography.
5. Identify anticlines and synclines in map view from the pattern of rock units and younging direction.

---

## Scope Boundaries

**Covers:** Topographic map reading (contours, relief, gradient); strike and dip measurement and notation; geological map conventions (formation colours, contacts, structural symbols); constructing and reading geological cross-sections; V-rule; fold recognition in map view (anticline vs. syncline from closure and younging).
**Does not cover:** Stereonet analysis, detailed structural geology (covered in 2.5.2), geophysical maps, remote sensing.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Seeing through the skin of the Earth"
**Hook:** A geological map is fundamentally different from every other kind of map you have used. A road map shows what's on the surface. A geological map shows what's *beneath* it — the rock types, their ages, their arrangement, and the structures that have deformed them, all collapsed onto a two-dimensional sheet. Every symbol on a geological map represents months or years of fieldwork by geologists who walked the ground, measured the rocks, and recorded what they found. Learning to read a geological map is learning to reconstruct a three-dimensional subsurface from a two-dimensional surface pattern — a skill that is central to exploration geology, engineering geology, hazard assessment, and fundamental research alike.

**Key terms:** topographic contour, strike, dip, formation, geological contact, cross-section, V-rule, anticline, syncline, geological map.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Maps, symbols, and what they mean"

#### Topographic maps
- **Contour lines** connect points of equal elevation. Rules: (1) contours never cross each other; (2) closely spaced contours indicate steep terrain; widely spaced indicate gentle terrain; (3) contours form V-shapes that point upstream (uphill) where they cross a river valley; (4) closed contours indicate a hill (or depression if marked with tick marks).
- **Contour interval**: the vertical distance between adjacent contours; must be stated on the map. A 10 m interval means each contour is 10 m above or below the next.
- **Reading relief**: the total range from lowest to highest point in the map area.

#### Strike and dip
- **Strike**: the compass bearing of a horizontal line drawn on the surface of a tilted rock layer. Measured with a compass clinometer. Expressed as a bearing (e.g., N045°E, or simply 045° in the right-hand rule convention) or as a cardinal direction pair (e.g., NE-SW). Strike tells you the *orientation* of the layer in the horizontal plane.
- **Dip**: the maximum angle (in degrees) at which the layer is inclined from the horizontal, measured perpendicular to strike, in the downslope direction. A dip of 0° = horizontal; 90° = vertical. Always recorded with direction (e.g., 35°SE means the layer dips at 35 degrees toward the southeast).
- **Strike-and-dip symbol**: a T-shaped symbol on geological maps. The long bar = strike direction; the short tick = dip direction; the number adjacent = dip angle. A circle with no tick = horizontal; a box with no number = vertical.

#### Geological map conventions
- **Formation colours**: each rock unit (formation, group, or member) is assigned a standard colour or pattern. The ICS has recommended colours for specific systems (periods); national surveys may vary.
- **Contact types**: solid line = certain contact (well-exposed, accurately located); dashed = approximate (covered by soil/vegetation but position estimated from float or topography); dotted = inferred or covered.
- **Fault symbols**: thick solid line = fault; teeth on the upper plate of a thrust fault; arrows for strike-slip sense.
- **Unconformity symbol**: wavy or irregular line between two units with a time gap.
- **Fossils, mines, springs**: standardised point symbols vary by survey but are always given in the legend.

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading structure in the map"

#### The V-rule and cross-sections
- **The V-rule** predicts how a geological contact (the surface trace of a rock layer boundary) crosses a valley or ridge: (1) *Horizontal layers* follow topographic contours exactly — the contact V's identically to the topographic contours. (2) *Vertical layers* cross topography as a straight line — no V at all. (3) *Dipping layers* V upstream (in the direction of dip) when dipping in the same direction as the stream's flow but less steeply than the valley walls; V downstream (opposite to dip direction) when dipping upstream, or when dipping downstream at a steeper angle than the valley floor.
- **Constructing a cross-section**: (1) Choose a line of section (usually perpendicular to dominant strike). (2) Draw the topographic profile along that line using contour intersections. (3) Project the surface contacts vertically down to their position on the profile line. (4) Draw the dipping surfaces downward at their measured dip angle from each contact point. (5) Connect contacts, respecting continuity of layers and any faults.

#### Anticlines and synclines in map view
- In a flat-topped or eroded landscape, folded rocks produce a striped map pattern. The key to distinguishing anticlines from synclines: (1) **Closure direction**: if the stripes close toward a point (younger rocks wrap around older), that point is a **pericline** — an anticline closes toward its oldest rocks in the core; a syncline closes toward its youngest rocks in the core. (2) **Younging direction**: in an undisturbed sequence, beds young upward (stratigraphy); in map view an anticline has the oldest formation in the centre, newer formations on either side; a syncline has the youngest formation in the centre, older on either side.

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. On a geological map, a rock contact is drawn as a dashed line. What does this indicate about the location and confidence of that contact, and why might it be recorded this way in the field?
2. A geologist measures the strike of a sandstone bed as 060° and the dip as 25°SE. Explain what each of these measurements means physically. If you were standing on the bed and looking along strike, which way would the rock slope down?
3. Explain the V-rule. A gently dipping limestone bed (dipping east at 15°) crosses a river valley that also drains to the east. Which direction does the contact V in the valley — upstream (west) or downstream (east)?
4. On a geological map you see a series of concentric elliptical outcrops. The centre of the ellipse exposes Ordovician rocks, and progressively younger (Silurian, Devonian) rocks occur outward. Is this an anticline or a syncline? How do you know?
5. You are constructing a cross-section across a north-dipping sequence of rocks. The surface trace of the contact shows a clear V pointing north (upstream) where it crosses a south-draining valley. Is this consistent with the measured north dip? Apply the V-rule to explain your answer.
