## Learning Objectives

1. Define the Cambrian Explosion and describe what appears in the fossil record at ~541–520 Ma.
2. Describe the Ediacaran biota and explain what it tells us about animal life before the Cambrian.
3. Explain small shelly fossils and why their appearance matters for understanding the onset of biomineralisation.
4. Distinguish stem groups from crown groups and apply these concepts to Cambrian animal body plans.
5. Evaluate competing hypotheses for the cause of the Cambrian Explosion (environmental, ecological, genetic) and explain what molecular clock evidence suggests about the true timing of animal divergences.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The Big Bang of animal evolution"
**Hook:** For most of Earth's 4.6-billion-year history, nothing visible to the naked eye existed. Microbial mats, single-celled organisms, and chemical traces. Then, within a geologically brief interval of roughly 20 million years beginning at ~541 Ma, almost every major animal body plan that has ever existed appeared in the fossil record. The Cambrian Explosion is not a gradual accumulation; it is a step change — a before and after so stark that it was used to define the base of the Phanerozoic Eon (the "era of visible life"). Understanding why it happened — and whether it is as sudden as it appears — is one of the most contested and productive questions in all of palaeontology.

**Key terms:** Cambrian Explosion, Ediacaran biota, small shelly fossils (SSF), biomineralisation, body plan (Bauplan), stem group, crown group, molecular clock.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Before the explosion: the Ediacaran world"

#### The Ediacaran Period (635–541 Ma)
- Named for the Ediacara Hills (South Australia, discovered 1946); the Ediacaran Period is the first formally named geological period defined by its fossil content
- Ediacaran organisms are macroscopic (up to 1 m), preserved as impressions in microbial mat-coated seafloor (Ediacaran "death mask" taphonomy) — microbial mats trapped and mineralised impressions of organisms resting on the surface
- Key Ediacaran organisms: **Dickinsonia** (flat, segmented, oval organism up to 1.4 m; recent biomarker analysis found cholesterol, consistent with animal identity); **Charnia** (frond-like, possibly a rangeomorph — a now-extinct lineage with fractal branching); **Kimberella** (bilateral, possibly a mollusc-grade bilaterian; trace fossils of feeding scratches associated); **Tribrachidium** (triradially symmetrical, sessile); **Aspidella** (disc-like holdfast)
- Debate: are Ediacaran organisms animals? Some clearly are (Dickinsonia, Kimberella). Others may represent entirely extinct kingdoms. Most specialists now accept that the Ediacaran biota includes a mix of early animals, proto-animals, and potentially entirely separate clades with no living descendants
- The end-Ediacaran extinction (~541 Ma): Ediacaran organisms almost entirely disappear at the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary — replaced by the Cambrian shelly fauna; some likely became extinct, but others may have survived unrecognised in the Cambrian soft-tissue record

#### Small shelly fossils (SSF, ~535–521 Ma)
- The first hard parts in the animal fossil record appear in the Early Cambrian as a diverse assemblage of tiny (0.1–2 mm) mineralized structures: tubes, cones, plates, scales, and spines
- Key SSF taxa: **Cloudina** (~548 Ma — actually the oldest known bilaterian with a hard skeleton, from the latest Ediacaran); **Anabarites** (triradially symmetrical tube); **Halkieria** (scale-covered animal, later shown to be a stem-group brachiopod); **tommotiids** (phosphatic sclerites of uncertain affinity)
- SSF represent the initial, experimental phase of biomineralisation — many different animal lineages independently evolved hard parts within a few million years of each other, using different minerals (calcite, aragonite, phosphate) and different structural strategies
- Cards: The Ediacaran Biota (Layers, BRAND.gold), Small Shelly Fossils and Biomineralisation (Mountain, BRAND.coral)

### Section 3 — Application: "The explosion and its causes"

#### The Cambrian Explosion (~541–520 Ma)
- Within ~20 Ma of the Precambrian–Cambrian boundary, representatives of most living animal phyla appear in the fossil record — arthropods, molluscs, echinoderms, brachiopods, chordates, annelids, porifera, cnidarians, and many more
- **Stem groups vs. crown groups**: a crown group is the last common ancestor of all living members of a clade plus all its descendants; a stem group is the set of extinct lineages more closely related to that crown group than to any other — they share some but not all of the derived features of the crown; Cambrian animals are mostly stem-group representatives of modern phyla (e.g., Anomalocaris is a stem-group arthropod — more closely related to arthropods than to molluscs, but outside the crown that includes living arthropods); this means Cambrian body-plan diversity represents the early morphological exploration of each major lineage before they acquired the full derived features of modern taxa

#### Competing hypotheses for the Cambrian Explosion
1. **Environmental/geochemical**: Neoproterozoic "Snowball Earth" glaciations (750–635 Ma) were followed by warm conditions; post-glacial phosphate and calcium flooding of oceans enabled biomineralisation; rising atmospheric oxygen (reaching modern-comparable levels in early Cambrian) enabled more energetically demanding animal body plans and more active metabolisms; oxygenation of deep oceans allowed colonisation of new habitats
2. **Ecological**: the "arms race" hypothesis — predation evolving in the Cambrian created selection pressure for defensive hard parts (shells, exoskeletons, spines); once hard parts appeared in predators, prey evolved hard parts defensively; positive feedback amplified diversification; also: the evolution of filter-feeding increased food availability for other organisms; ecosystem engineering by burrowing organisms created new niches
3. **Genetic/developmental**: evolution of the Hox gene toolkit and other developmental regulatory networks enabled modular body plans with independently evolvable segments, appendages, and organs; molecular homology shows the same regulatory genes (Hox genes) pattern the body axes of insects, vertebrates, molluscs, and annelids — suggesting this toolkit was in place by the latest Ediacaran
4. **Molecular clock evidence**: estimates of animal divergence times from DNA sequence comparisons, calibrated by fossil dates, consistently place the divergence of major animal phyla at 650–800 Ma — 100–200 Ma before the fossil record begins; this suggests a long "cryptic" interval of soft-bodied animal diversification invisible in the body fossil record; the Cambrian "explosion" is partly an explosion of fossil preservability (biomineralisation) not of phylogenetic diversification
- Card: Causes of the Cambrian Explosion (Clock, BRAND.amethyst)

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. What is the Cambrian Explosion, and why is it considered one of the most significant events in the history of animal life?
2. Describe the Ediacaran biota and explain why identifying these organisms as "animals" is contested. What type of evidence (other than body fossils) supports the animal interpretation for some Ediacaran organisms?
3. What are small shelly fossils (SSF), and why does their appearance across multiple animal lineages simultaneously suggest about the Cambrian Explosion?
4. Distinguish between a stem group and a crown group, and explain why most Cambrian animals are classified as stem-group representatives of living phyla rather than members of the crown groups.
5. Molecular clock analyses consistently place the divergence of major animal phyla at 650–800 Ma, long before the Cambrian Explosion at ~541 Ma. How do palaeontologists reconcile this discrepancy, and what does it imply about the nature of the "explosion"?
