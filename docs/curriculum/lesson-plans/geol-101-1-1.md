## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Identify the four major layers of Earth's interior** — crust, mantle, outer core, inner core — and recall the approximate depth or thickness of each.
2. **Explain how seismic waves provide the primary evidence for Earth's internal structure**, including the difference between P-waves and S-waves and what their behavior tells us about each layer's state of matter.
3. **Distinguish chemical layering** (crust, mantle, core — defined by composition) **from mechanical layering** (lithosphere and asthenosphere — defined by how the rock behaves).
4. **Describe at least one independent line of evidence beyond seismology** for the deep interior (e.g., Earth's bulk density, meteorite composition, or moment-of-inertia constraints).

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "We've never seen it"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** The deepest hole humans have ever drilled — the Kola Superdeep Borehole — reached only ~12 km, while Earth's radius is 6,371 km. That's about 1/500th of the way through. So how do we know what's down there? The answer is that Earth itself rings like a bell every time there's an earthquake, and seismographs around the world record the music.

**What this section establishes.**
- The scale problem (12 km drilled vs. 6,371 km radius).
- The setup for seismology as the primary evidence stream.
- The promise that the lesson will reveal what's inside and how we know.

**Key terms introduced (with definitions):**
- **Crust** — the thin, rocky outermost layer.
- **Mantle** — the thick, hot, mostly solid silicate layer beneath the crust.
- **Core** — the dense, iron-rich center, divided into a liquid outer core and solid inner core.
- **Seismic wave** — a wave of energy released by an earthquake that travels through Earth's interior.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The four layers and the evidence
**Type:** `concept` (with cards for the four layers)
**Estimated reading time:** ~7–9 minutes

**Subsection A — The four layers.** Walk through each layer in order, with thickness/depth, composition, state, and one signature feature each.
- **Crust** (~0–70 km thick): solid silicate rock, where we live, the thinnest layer.
- **Mantle** (~2,900 km thick): solid silicate rock, very hot, slow-flowing on geologic timescales.
- **Outer core** (~2,200 km thick): liquid iron-nickel alloy.
- **Inner core** (~1,200 km radius): solid iron-nickel alloy.

**Subsection B — How we know: seismic waves.**
- **P-waves** (compressional, "primary"): travel through solid AND liquid.
- **S-waves** (shear, "secondary"): travel through solid ONLY — they cannot pass through liquid.
- The **S-wave shadow zone** beyond ~104° from an earthquake is the diagnostic evidence that Earth's outer core is liquid. (This is the single most important conceptual takeaway.)
- The Moho (Mohorovičić discontinuity) marks the seismic boundary between crust and mantle.

**Subsection C — Two ways to slice the Earth.**
- **Chemical layering** (composition): crust / mantle / core — used when asking "what is it made of?"
- **Mechanical layering** (behavior): lithosphere (rigid; crust + uppermost mantle) / asthenosphere (slow-flowing upper mantle). Used when asking "how does it move?"
- The student should leave knowing these are *two different maps of the same Earth*, not two different Earths. This sets up plate tectonics in Module 1.4.

**Subsection D — Beyond seismology (brief).** One short paragraph noting that Earth's bulk density (~5.5 g/cm³, much higher than surface rocks at ~2.7 g/cm³) and the iron-nickel composition of meteorites independently support a dense metallic core — seismology is the strongest evidence, but not the only evidence.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3
**Format:** 4-option multiple choice with explanation for the correct answer (per master plan rule that quizzes include explanations).

Question topics planned:
1. Layer identification (which layer is which based on a description of state/composition).
2. Seismic evidence (why the S-wave shadow zone proves the outer core is liquid).
3. Chemical vs. mechanical layering (why "the lithosphere is the same as the crust" is wrong).

Each answer choice — correct and distractors — will receive a one-sentence explanation per the lesson production rules.

## 3. Key terms introduced

| Term | First introduced in | Definition planned |
|---|---|---|
| Crust | Intro | Earth's thin, rocky outer layer; ranges from ~7 km (oceanic) to ~70 km (continental) — though the ocean/continent distinction is the subject of Lesson 1.1.2 and will not be elaborated here. |
| Mantle | Intro | The ~2,900-km-thick layer of hot, mostly solid silicate rock between the crust and the core. |
| Outer core | Concept | The ~2,200-km-thick layer of molten iron-nickel alloy surrounding the inner core. |
| Inner core | Concept | The solid iron-nickel sphere at Earth's center, ~1,200 km in radius. |
| Seismic wave | Intro | A wave of mechanical energy released by an earthquake that travels through Earth's interior. |
| P-wave | Concept | Compressional ("primary") seismic wave; travels through solids and liquids. |
| S-wave | Concept | Shear ("secondary") seismic wave; travels through solids only. |
| Shadow zone | Concept | The angular range beyond ~104° from an earthquake where direct S-waves are not detected — evidence for a liquid outer core. |
| Moho (Mohorovičić discontinuity) | Concept | The seismic boundary that separates the crust from the underlying mantle. |
| Lithosphere | Concept | The rigid mechanical layer composed of the crust plus the cold uppermost mantle. |
| Asthenosphere | Concept | The slow-flowing upper-mantle layer beneath the lithosphere. |

This is the working glossary; the lesson copy will not necessarily define every term in a separate panel — some will be defined inline.

## 4. Source list

All sources are Tier 1 (US government scientific agencies) or Tier 2 (open-licensed academic content), as required by GEOL 101 (100-level course).

1. **USGS — "This Dynamic Earth: The Story of Plate Tectonics"** (Kious & Tilling). USGS General Interest Publication. Tier 1.
   *Use:* Foundation reference for Earth's layered interior, the four-layer model, and the role of seismic data in revealing internal structure.
   *URL:* https://pubs.usgs.gov/gip/dynamic/dynamic.html

2. **USGS Earthquake Hazards Program — Seismic waves and Earth's interior.** Tier 1.
   *Use:* Specifics on P-wave vs. S-wave behavior, S-wave shadow zone, and how seismic data constrain layer states.
   *URL:* https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards

3. **NASA Solar System Exploration / Earth — "By the Numbers."** Tier 1.
   *Use:* Earth's bulk physical parameters (radius, mass, density) for the "beyond seismology" subsection.
   *URL:* https://science.nasa.gov/earth/facts/

4. **OpenStax — *Physical Geology*, 2nd ed.** (CC-BY 4.0). Tier 2.
   *Use:* Chapter coverage of Earth's interior, the Moho, and the lithosphere/asthenosphere mechanical distinction. Useful for cross-checking the chemical-vs.-mechanical layering framing.
   *URL:* https://openstax.org/books/physical-geology-2e

5. **UCAR Center for Science Education — "Earth's Interior" learning resource.** Tier 2.
   *Use:* Layer-by-layer descriptions and an accessible 100-level vocabulary check.
   *URL:* https://scied.ucar.edu/learning-zone/earth/earths-interior

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

These are the conceptual hooks the lesson is built around. Each section of the draft will resolve one or more of them.

1. **"We've never seen below 12 km — so how do we know what's inside Earth?"**
   *Resolved by:* The intro hook (Kola borehole vs. Earth radius) and the concept-section coverage of seismic waves as Earth's CT scan.

2. **"What are the four layers of Earth, and what makes them different from each other?"**
   *Resolved by:* The four-layers walkthrough in the concept section, including thickness, composition, and physical state.

3. **"Why do geologists draw two different maps of Earth's layers — chemical and mechanical — and is one of them wrong?"**
   *Resolved by:* Subsection C of the concept section. Neither is wrong — they answer different questions. This subsection also seeds the plate tectonics framework that returns in Module 1.4.

## 6. Connection to surrounding lessons

### Previous lesson
**This is the first lesson in GEOL 101.** No previous lesson exists in the course. The lesson should therefore not assume any prior earth-science vocabulary and must define its own foundational terms (which the plan does — see §3).

### Next lesson — 1.1.2: The Crust — Continental vs. Oceanic
Lesson 1.1.1 introduces the crust as the thinnest, outermost chemical layer and notes that it varies in thickness from a few kilometers under the oceans to several tens of kilometers under continents. **It will not explain why this difference exists** — that explanation (composition, density, isostasy) is the entire premise of Lesson 1.1.2.

The final paragraph of the concept section will explicitly hand off:
> *"In the next lesson, we'll zoom into the crust and find out why a thick continental block sits high above a thin slab of ocean floor — and why both are 'crust' but made of different things."*

### Forward dependency map (for course coherence)
- **1.1.2 (next)** depends on: the four-layer model and the concept that the crust varies.
- **1.1.3 (mantle convection)** depends on: the asthenosphere existing as a slow-flowing layer (introduced here as part of mechanical layering, but not elaborated).
- **1.1.4 (core and magnetic field)** depends on: the inner/outer core distinction being established here, including outer-core liquidity (the geodynamo will be explained in 1.1.4, not here).
- **Module 1.4 (plate tectonics)** depends on: the lithosphere being defined as a rigid mechanical unit that floats on the asthenosphere.

This lesson opens those threads but does not pull on them. Each subsequent lesson can begin with: *"In Lesson 1.1.1 we established X — now let's look at Y in detail."*
