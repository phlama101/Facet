## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Explain why the mantle convects** — identify residual heat from planetary formation and ongoing radioactive decay as the two heat sources driving the circulation.
2. **Describe how solid rock can flow** — articulate why the mantle, despite being solid, deforms and circulates over millions of years (viscous flow under heat and pressure), without invoking any equation.
3. **Trace the convection cycle** — describe the path of a parcel of hot mantle rock: rises because it is less dense → cools near the surface → becomes denser → sinks → reheats → rises again.
4. **Connect mantle convection to the surface observations introduced in 1.1.2** — explain that rising convection draws lithospheric plates apart (creating new oceanic crust) and that sinking convection pulls old oceanic crust back into the mantle (explaining why no ocean floor is older than ~200 Ma).
5. **Name at least one line of evidence** for mantle convection (heat-flow measurements or seismic tomography).

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "The slow boil"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Watch a pot of thick oatmeal on a stove. Slow domes of porridge rise from the bottom, spread across the surface, cool, and sink back down at the edges. This is convection — the circulation of a fluid driven by heat from below. Earth's mantle does something strikingly similar, except the "fluid" is solid rock, the "pot" is 2,900 km deep, and one complete circulation cycle takes tens to hundreds of millions of years.

Lessons 1.1.1 and 1.1.2 established two facts that need explaining: (1) the mantle is hot, mostly solid, and "slow-flowing on geologic timescales," and (2) oceanic crust is continuously created and destroyed — something is driving that cycle. This lesson identifies the engine: **mantle convection**.

**What this section establishes.**
- The oatmeal / convection analogy as an accessible mental model.
- The two open threads from previous lessons (mantle flows; oceanic crust is recycled) converge here.
- Key vocabulary for the concept section.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Mantle convection** — the slow, heat-driven circulation of rock within Earth's mantle, in which hotter, less dense material rises and cooler, denser material sinks.
- **Viscosity** — a material's resistance to flow. Honey is more viscous than water. The mantle is enormously more viscous than either, but it still flows given enough time and temperature.
- **Heat flow** — the rate at which heat from Earth's interior escapes through the surface, measured in milliwatts per square metre. Higher values indicate hotter material rising beneath.
- **Radioactive decay** — the process by which unstable atomic nuclei (uranium, thorium, potassium-40) break apart and release heat. This ongoing decay within the mantle and crust is one of two major heat sources driving convection.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: How rock flows and what it moves
**Type:** `concept` (with three cards)
**Estimated reading time:** ~7–9 minutes

**Subsection A — Solid, but flowing.** A common misconception is that the mantle must be liquid in order to flow. It is not — it is solid rock. But "solid" does not mean "rigid forever." Given extremely high temperatures and pressures, and given enough time, solid rock deforms the way glass does: slowly, continuously, without fracturing. This is called **solid-state creep**. On a human timescale, mantle rock appears completely rigid. On a timescale of millions of years, it circulates like an extremely thick fluid. The asthenosphere (the upper part of the mantle, introduced in 1.1.1) is where this flow is easiest — it is hot enough and under enough pressure to deform relatively readily compared to the deeper mantle.

**Subsection B — Why the mantle is hot.** Two heat sources keep the mantle hot enough to convect:
1. **Residual heat** from Earth's formation (~4.54 billion years ago). The violent collisions that assembled Earth generated enormous heat. The planet has been cooling ever since — but it is so large that significant heat remains trapped in the interior.
2. **Radioactive decay** within the mantle and crust. Elements such as uranium, thorium, and potassium-40 decay slowly and continuously release heat. This ongoing process supplements the original heat of formation.

Together, these sources maintain the temperature gradient between the hot deep interior and the cooler surface — the gradient that drives convection.

**Subsection C — The convection cycle.** The circulation follows a predictable path:
- Deep in the mantle, rock is heated by the core and by radioactive decay. It expands slightly, becoming less dense than the surrounding rock.
- The less dense rock rises slowly — not as a single blob, but as broad upwelling currents that may be thousands of kilometres wide.
- As it rises and approaches the surface, pressure decreases and the rock loses heat to the lithosphere and ultimately to the surface. It cools, contracts, and becomes denser.
- The denser rock sinks back into the deep mantle, where it is reheated and the cycle repeats.
- This creates **convection cells** — paired regions of rising and sinking material. The scale is planetary: individual cells may span thousands of kilometres.

**Subsection D — What convection does at the surface.** This is the payoff that ties back to Lesson 1.1.2:
- Where hot mantle rises, it pushes up on the lithosphere above and drives the plates apart. New oceanic crust forms in the gap — this is why mid-ocean ridges exist and why they are elevated above the surrounding ocean floor.
- Where cold mantle sinks, it pulls the overlying plate downward and back into the mantle. Dense oceanic crust follows. This is subduction — the mechanism that destroys old oceanic crust and explains why none of it is older than ~200 Ma.
- Continental crust, being too buoyant to subduct easily, accumulates and persists — which is why continental rocks can be billions of years old.
- The full plate-boundary story (divergent, convergent, transform) is the subject of Module 1.4; this lesson only establishes that convection is the driving engine.

**Subsection E — Evidence: how we know.** Two main lines:
1. **Heat-flow measurements**: instruments on the seafloor and in continental drill holes measure how much heat is escaping. Mid-ocean ridges — where hot mantle upwells — have significantly higher heat flow than old, stable ocean floor or continental interiors. This spatial pattern matches what convection predicts.
2. **Seismic tomography**: by measuring the speed of seismic waves through the mantle from thousands of earthquakes and recording stations, geophysicists can image the mantle's interior much as a medical CT scan images the human body. Hot rock slows seismic waves; cold rock speeds them up. Tomographic images show plumes of hot material rising and slabs of cold material sinking — direct confirmation that convection is occurring.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Heat sources** — which two sources drive mantle convection? (residual heat from formation + radioactive decay — distractors include solar heating and volcanic eruptions)
2. **Solid-state flow** — how can the mantle be solid and yet flow? (viscous creep under heat and pressure over millions of years — distractors include the mantle being liquid, or the mantle not actually moving)
3. **Surface consequences** — what happens at a location where hot mantle material rises toward the surface? (plates are pushed apart, new oceanic crust forms at a mid-ocean ridge — distractors involve incorrect surface features)

Each answer choice — correct and all distractors — receives an explanation within the single `explain` field.

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"The mantle is solid — how can it possibly flow?"**
   *Resolved by:* The solid-state creep explanation in Subsection A — solid rock deforms over millions of years under extreme heat and pressure without melting.

2. **"Where does the heat inside Earth come from? Why hasn't it all leaked out yet?"**
   *Resolved by:* Subsection B — two heat sources (residual formation heat + ongoing radioactive decay) replenish the supply; Earth's size means cooling is extremely slow.

3. **"How does what happens deep in the mantle affect what we see at the surface?"**
   *Resolved by:* Subsection D — rising convection creates mid-ocean ridges and new oceanic crust; sinking convection drives subduction and explains why ocean floor is never older than ~200 Ma.
