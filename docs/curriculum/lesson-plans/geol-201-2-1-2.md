## Learning Objectives

1. State and apply all six classical principles of relative dating (superposition, original horizontality, lateral continuity, cross-cutting relationships, inclusions, faunal succession).
2. Use these principles to reconstruct the sequence of geological events from a stratigraphic cross-section or map.
3. Distinguish conformable sequences from unconformities (angular, disconformity, nonconformity) and explain what each represents.
4. Explain the concept of correlation and how it allows local sequences to be connected into a global relative timescale.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Reading strata like pages in a book"
**Hook:** William Smith, a self-educated canal surveyor, spent 24 years criss-crossing England examining rock exposures in canal cuts, quarries, and cliffs. In 1815 he published the first geological map of a country — A Delineation of the Strata of England and Wales — an astonishing achievement that required no laboratory equipment, no radiometric dating, and no university education. Smith's method was simple: each rock formation contains a characteristic assemblage of fossils, and that assemblage does not repeat. By matching fossils across exposures separated by hundreds of kilometres, he could demonstrate that geologically unconnected outcrops belonged to the same layer. The principles he used — and which Steno had formalised 150 years earlier — are still the foundation of every stratigraphic analysis performed today.

**Key terms:** superposition, original horizontality, cross-cutting relationships, inclusions, faunal succession, unconformity, correlation, lithostratigraphy, biostratigraphy.

### Section 2 — Concept: "The six principles of relative dating"

#### Steno's three principles (1669)
- **Superposition:** In an undisturbed sequence, the oldest layers are at the bottom and younger layers are above; a layer is younger than the layers beneath it and older than the layers above it. The single most-used principle in stratigraphy.
- **Original horizontality:** Sediment is deposited in nearly horizontal layers (controlled by gravity); tilted or folded strata were originally horizontal and have been subsequently deformed.
- **Lateral continuity:** A sedimentary layer was originally continuous in all directions until it thinned out at its margins or was interrupted by a barrier; two exposures of similar rock at the same level in a sequence can be presumed to have been part of the same original layer.

#### Three additional principles
- **Cross-cutting relationships:** Any geological feature that cuts across another must be younger than the feature it cuts. Intrusions, faults, and erosion surfaces are all younger than what they cut through. Critical for dating igneous intrusions relative to surrounding strata.
- **Inclusions:** A fragment included in a rock must be older than the rock that contains it. If a granite contains inclusions of schist, the schist is older. If a conglomerate contains pebbles of granite, the granite is older than the conglomerate.
- **Faunal succession (William Smith / Georges Cuvier):** Fossil assemblages succeed one another in a predictable, non-repeating order through the rock record. Species appear, persist for a geological interval, and go extinct; the same sequence of appearances and extinctions is preserved in rocks worldwide. This allows rocks of the same age to be recognised even when they are composed of different rock types.

#### Unconformities — gaps in the record
- **Angular unconformity:** Tilted or folded strata overlain by younger, more gently dipping strata; implies at least one episode of deposition, lithification, deformation, uplift, erosion, and renewed deposition (Hutton's Siccar Point)
- **Disconformity:** Erosion surface between two parallel (sub-horizontal) sequences; layers below and above are parallel but an erosional surface is present; represents a period of non-deposition or erosion without deformation
- **Nonconformity:** Sedimentary rocks overlying crystalline basement (igneous or metamorphic rock); the contact records a major episode of erosion that exposed deep crustal material before renewed sedimentation

#### Correlation
- **Lithostratigraphy:** Correlating rock units by rock type and position; works over short distances where rock types can be traced laterally
- **Biostratigraphy:** Correlating by fossil content (faunal succession); works over vast distances because the same species succession appears worldwide; basis for William Smith's map
- **Chemostratigraphy:** Correlating by chemical signatures (δ¹³C, δ¹⁸O, Sr isotopes); useful where biostratigraphy is limited (Precambrian) — introduced here, developed further in Module 2.2

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading a cross-section step by step"

#### A worked example
- Present a conceptual cross-section with: horizontal limestone layers (A, B, C), tilted shale (D) beneath an angular unconformity, a granite intrusion (E) cutting through D, a diabase dike (F) cutting through A–C and E, and a fault (G) displacing layers A–C
- Step-by-step application of all six principles to build the full sequence of events
- Expected sequence: D deposited first (oldest) → D tilted and uplifted → E intruded into D → D eroded → A, B, C deposited on unconformity surface → F intruded (cuts A–C and E) → G faulted (cuts all layers including F)

#### Real examples
- Grand Canyon cross-section: Vishnu Schist (metamorphic basement, >1.7 Ga) → Great Unconformity (gap of ~1 billion years) → Tapeats Sandstone (Cambrian, ~508 Ma) → conformable Paleozoic sequence above → Kaibab Limestone (Permian, ~270 Ma) at rim; cross-cutting granite intrusions in the Vishnu provide relative and radiometric dates; the Great Unconformity is one of Earth's most dramatic disconformities/angular unconformities
- Scottish Highlands: Lewisian Gneiss (Archean metamorphic basement, ~2.7 Ga) → nonconformity → Torridonian Sandstone (Proterozoic, ~1.0 Ga) → disconformity → Cambrian quartzites; demonstrates three different unconformity types in a single field area

#### The limits of relative dating
- Relative dating tells you sequence, not duration
- A thick sequence of strata may represent 10 Ma or 100 Ma; without radiometric ages, you cannot know
- Unconformities are particularly dangerous — a surface that looks like a simple bedding plane may represent 500 Ma of missing time (as the Great Unconformity does in some locations)
- The combination of relative sequencing + radiometric dates is necessary for a complete stratigraphic picture (developed in 2.1.3 and 2.1.4)

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. A granite intrusion cuts through horizontal limestone beds. A basalt dike cuts through both the granite and the limestone. Rank these three rock bodies from oldest to youngest and state which principle(s) you applied.
2. What is the difference between an angular unconformity and a disconformity? What geological events must have occurred to produce each?
3. William Smith could correlate rock formations across England without any chemical analysis or laboratory equipment. What principle made this possible?
4. A cross-section shows Cambrian sandstone directly overlying Archean granite, with no deformed sedimentary rock in between. What type of unconformity is this and what does it represent?
5. A geologist finds a conglomerate containing pebbles of red sandstone, grey limestone, and black basalt. The matrix cementing them is a white quartz sandstone. Which rock is definitely the youngest in this group, and why?
