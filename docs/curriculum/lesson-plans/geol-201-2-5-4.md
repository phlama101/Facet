## Learning Objectives

1. Apply the Mohs hardness scale to determine the hardness of an unknown mineral using field tools (fingernail, coin, knife, glass).
2. Use streak, cleavage, lustre, and the acid test to distinguish minerals that appear similar in colour or texture.
3. Classify an igneous rock hand sample as plutonic or volcanic based on texture, and identify it as felsic, intermediate, or mafic based on mineral content.
4. Identify a sedimentary rock in hand sample by grain size, sorting, and the presence or absence of carbonate (acid test), distinguishing clastic from chemical/biochemical types.
5. Recognise the key metamorphic textures — slaty cleavage, schistosity, and gneissic banding — and relate each to metamorphic grade.

---

## Scope Boundaries

**Covers:** Mohs hardness test using field tools; streak, cleavage, lustre, and acid test; identification of the 8 most common rock-forming minerals; igneous rock classification by texture and mineralogy; sedimentary rock classification by grain size, sorting, and composition; metamorphic rock classification by fabric (cleavage, schistosity, gneissic banding) and index minerals.
**Does not cover:** Optical mineralogy (thin sections), geochemical classification schemes (TAS diagram, QAPF modal analysis), detailed metamorphic petrology.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The field geologist's toolkit"
**Hook:** In a laboratory you have access to X-ray diffractometers, electron microprobes, and mass spectrometers. In the field, you have your eyes, a hand lens (×10 loupe), a knife, a coin, a piece of streak plate, a small bottle of dilute HCl, and everything you have learnt about geology. Field identification is not guesswork — it is a systematic process of observing properties in a defined sequence and narrowing the possibilities. It is the first analytical step in every field campaign, the one that tells you what rocks you are dealing with before any sample even reaches a laboratory. This lesson teaches that process for minerals, igneous rocks, sedimentary rocks, and metamorphic rocks.

**Key terms:** Mohs hardness, streak, cleavage, lustre, acid test, specific gravity, hand sample, texture (igneous), foliation, index mineral, metamorphic grade.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Mineral identification — properties and tests"

#### The Mohs hardness scale
- Hardness measures resistance to scratching: Talc 1, Gypsum 2, Calcite 3, Fluorite 4, Apatite 5, K-feldspar (Orthoclase) 6, Quartz 7, Topaz 8, Corundum 9, Diamond 10.
- **Field tools**: fingernail ~2.5; copper coin ~3; glass plate ~5.5; steel pocket-knife blade ~5.5; tungsten carbide (hardness card) ~9.
- **Practical method**: scratch the unknown with each tool in sequence; the lowest-hardness tool that leaves a mark determines the upper bound. Always clean the surface and confirm it is a true scratch, not powder from the tool.
- **Key discriminations**: (1) calcite (3) vs. quartz (7): quartz cannot be scratched by a knife; calcite can. (2) gypsum (2) vs. muscovite (~2.5): both soft, but gypsum has a distinctive pearly lustre on cleavage faces. (3) feldspar (6) vs. quartz (7): a steel knife barely scratches feldspar but cannot scratch quartz.

#### Streak, cleavage, and lustre
- **Streak**: the colour of the powdered mineral on a streak plate (unglazed porcelain, hardness ~6.5). More consistent than surface colour because it is unaffected by surface oxidation and reflects the intrinsic mineral chemistry. Haematite: red-brown streak despite variable grey-to-red surface colour. Pyrite: black streak despite metallic gold surface appearance. Magnetite: black streak; magnetic. Quartz: white streak (harder than streak plate — produces powder from plate).
- **Cleavage**: tendency to break along flat planes controlled by crystal structure (weak bonds). Number of planes and angle between them are diagnostic: halite = three planes at 90° (cubic); calcite = three planes at 75°/105° (rhombohedral); amphibole = two planes at 60°/120°; pyroxene = two planes at 87°/93°; feldspar = two planes at ~90°; muscovite/biotite = one perfect plane (basal). Fracture (non-planar) vs. cleavage is a key distinction.
- **Lustre**: the quality of light reflection. *Metallic* (opaque, mirror-like — sulphides, native metals); *vitreous* (glassy — quartz, feldspars); *resinous* (amber-like — sphalerite); *pearly* (pearl-like iridescence — micas, selenite gypsum); *silky* (fibrous — fibrous gypsum/selenite, asbestos); *adamantine* (brilliant — diamond, zircon).
- **Acid test**: one drop of 10% HCl on the mineral. *Vigorous effervescence* = calcite (CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CO₂ + H₂O + CaCl₂). *Slow effervescence only when powdered* = dolomite (less reactive than calcite). *No reaction* = quartz, feldspar, most silicates, sulphides.

### Section 3 — Application: "Identifying rocks in hand sample"

#### Igneous rocks
- **Texture is the primary classifier**: grain size reflects cooling rate.
  - *Coarse-grained (phaneritic)* — individual crystals visible to the naked eye, >1 mm; indicative of slow cooling at depth (plutonic). Granite (quartz + K-feldspar + plagioclase ± biotite/hornblende), gabbro (plagioclase + pyroxene ± olivine), diorite (plagioclase + hornblende).
  - *Fine-grained (aphanitic)* — crystals microscopic, <0.1 mm; rapid cooling at surface (volcanic). Rhyolite (felsic, light-coloured), andesite (intermediate, grey), basalt (mafic, dark grey to black).
  - *Porphyritic* — large crystals (phenocrysts) set in a fine-grained groundmass; two-stage cooling history (magma partially crystallised at depth, then erupted). Extremely common in volcanic rocks.
  - *Glassy* — no crystallinity; quenched melt. Obsidian (silicic, black); tachylite (basaltic glass, rare).
  - *Vesicular* — gas bubbles trapped on eruption; scoria (basaltic), pumice (rhyolitic — low density, floats).
- **Colour and mineralogy — felsic vs. mafic**: felsic rocks (granite, rhyolite) are light-coloured, rich in quartz and K-feldspar, Si-rich. Mafic rocks (gabbro, basalt) are dark, rich in pyroxene and olivine, Fe/Mg-rich. Intermediate rocks (diorite, andesite) are grey.

#### Sedimentary rocks
- **Clastic rocks**: classified by grain size (Wentworth scale). Conglomerate (>2 mm, rounded clasts), breccia (>2 mm, angular clasts — shorter transport), sandstone (0.063–2 mm), siltstone (0.004–0.063 mm), mudstone/shale (<0.004 mm — shale has fissility, breaks into thin sheets along bedding planes; mudstone is blocky).
- **Chemical and biochemical rocks**: *limestone* — CaCO₃; fizzes vigorously in HCl; can be bioclastic (shell fragments), micritic (mud-size carbonate grains), or sparitic (coarsely crystalline). *Dolomite* — CaMg(CO₃)₂; fizzes slowly unless powdered; often saddle-shaped crystals, mottled texture. *Chert* — microcrystalline SiO₂; extremely hard (7), conchoidal fracture, waxy lustre, no HCl reaction; occurs as nodules in limestone or as bedded layers (deep marine radiolarite). *Rock salt (halite)* — cubic cleavage, salty taste, very soft (2.5). *Coal* — black, light, greasy lustre, low density; high carbon content from compressed organic matter.

#### Metamorphic rocks
- **Non-foliated**: no fabric — result of contact metamorphism or certain protoliths. *Quartzite* (metamorphosed sandstone — interlocking quartz, very hard, no HCl reaction). *Marble* (metamorphosed limestone — interlocking calcite crystals, HCl reaction, variety of colours).
- **Foliated**: alignment of minerals produces a planar fabric. Grade from low to high:
  - *Slate* — very fine-grained, slaty cleavage (smooth planar fracture, not parallel to original bedding). Low grade (chlorite zone). Protolith = shale/mudstone.
  - *Phyllite* — fine-grained, silky sheen on cleavage surfaces (aligned sericite/chlorite). Slightly higher grade than slate.
  - *Schist* — medium to coarse-grained, strong schistosity (aligned micas visible). Index minerals indicate grade: chlorite (greenschist facies) → biotite → garnet → staurolite → kyanite → sillimanite (granulite facies).
  - *Gneiss* — coarse-grained, gneissic banding (alternating light felsic and dark mafic layers). High grade; migmatite at partial-melt conditions.

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. You find a dark-coloured mineral in a rock. It leaves a red-brown streak on the streak plate, scratches easily with a copper coin, and produces no reaction with HCl. What is the most likely mineral, and which three properties together identify it?
2. A rock sample is coarse-grained with crystals ~3–5 mm across, light grey, containing clear glassy quartz, pink K-feldspar, white plagioclase, and black biotite. Name the rock and explain which textural and mineralogical properties led to your identification.
3. Two rock samples both fizz in dilute HCl. Sample A fizzes vigorously when HCl is applied directly to the surface. Sample B only fizzes when the surface is first powdered with a knife. What are the two minerals, and what is the chemical reason for the difference in reactivity?
4. You are given three metamorphic rocks: one has a smooth planar fracture with a dull lustre and no visible crystals; one has a silky sheen on its cleavage surfaces; one has coarse foliation defined by aligned biotite and garnet porphyroblasts. Identify each rock, place them in order from lowest to highest metamorphic grade, and name the protolith most likely for the first two.
5. A volcanic rock contains ~3 mm hornblende and plagioclase crystals (phenocrysts) set in a fine-grained dark groundmass, with 10–20% vesicles filled by green chlorite. Identify the rock and explain what the texture tells you about its cooling history and what the vesicles tell you about the eruption conditions.
