## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Mountains from the deep"

**Hook:** The Andes are 7,000 km long, rise to nearly 7,000 m, and were built by an ocean floor that no longer exists. The Nazca Plate has been sliding beneath South America for over 200 million years — subducting into the mantle at rates of 7–8 cm per year, continuously feeding magma to the volcanic arc above. Every copper mine in Chile, every volcanic eruption in Ecuador, every deep earthquake in Peru traces back to a single slab of oceanic lithosphere descending silently through the mantle. Subduction is the most consequential process on Earth's surface: it recycles oceanic crust back into the mantle, builds continental crust from arc magmatism, generates the planet's largest earthquakes, and raises mountain ranges thousands of metres above sea level.

**Key Terms:**
- **Subduction** — The process by which a denser lithospheric plate descends beneath a less dense plate into the mantle at a convergent boundary. Subduction recycles oceanic crust and drives arc volcanism, deep seismicity, and orogenesis.
- **Accretionary wedge** — A mass of deformed sediment and oceanic crustal material scraped off the subducting plate and accreted (added) to the overriding plate's leading edge. Forms a chaotic, compressed wedge of rock at the inner wall of the ocean trench.
- **Volcanic arc** — A curved chain of volcanoes on the overriding plate, roughly parallel to the trench, fed by magma generated by water-fluxed melting of the mantle wedge above the subducting slab. Can be oceanic (island arc) or continental (continental arc).
- **Orogenesis** — The process of mountain building, typically at convergent plate boundaries. Two main types: subduction-related (Andean-type) and continental collision (Himalayan-type).
- **Wadati-Benioff zone** — The inclined zone of seismicity that traces the path of a subducting slab through the mantle. Earthquakes become progressively deeper with distance from the trench, reaching 670 km depth in some subduction zones.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Slab, melt, mountain"

**Body overview:**
- Subduction mechanics: slab bends at trench, descends at 30–70°, seismicity deepens along Wadati-Benioff zone
- Dehydration at depth: water-bearing minerals (serpentinite, amphibole, chlorite) release water at 100–150 km; water migrates upward into the mantle wedge
- Water-fluxed melting: water lowers the melting point of peridotite → partial melting → andesitic to basaltic arc magma → rises through overriding plate → volcanic arc
- Accretionary wedge: subducting plate carries oceanic sediment; most is scraped off and added to overriding plate
- Andean-type orogeny: trench offshore + accretionary wedge + volcanic arc (andesite/granite) + metamorphic belt + possible back-arc basin behind arc
- Himalayan-type orogeny: suture zone (remnant of closed ocean) + fold-thrust belt + crustal thickening + isostatic uplift + no arc + Tibetan Plateau as overthickened crust
- Rock types: subduction → andesite/dacite (arc volcanics), diorite/granodiorite/granite (arc plutons), blueschist (high-P low-T subduction metamorphism); collision → schist, gneiss, marble in mountain roots

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Subduction zones produce large volcanic eruptions even though the magma source is the mantle — which should already be hot enough to melt. What additional factor triggers melting specifically at subduction zones?
- A) The friction of the descending slab against the overriding plate generates enough heat to melt the mantle rock directly above the contact
- B) The subducting slab carries water-bearing minerals to depth; when those minerals break down, water is released into the mantle wedge above the slab; water lowers the melting point of the mantle rock, triggering partial melting and generating arc magma ✓
- C) The weight of the subducting slab compresses the mantle below, increasing pressure until the mantle rock melts — the same mechanism as at mid-ocean ridges
- D) Radioactive elements concentrated in oceanic sediment on the subducting plate decay and generate enough heat to melt the surrounding mantle

**Q2:** A geologist maps a chaotic zone of compressed, folded, and faulted sedimentary and oceanic rock on the landward side of an ocean trench. The rocks include fragments of deep-sea chert, basalt, and limestone in a sheared matrix. What is this feature, and how did it form?
- A) A volcanic arc — the compressed rocks record successive lava flows from arc volcanoes that built up over millions of years
- B) A back-arc basin — extension behind the volcanic arc caused the crust to pull apart, creating a basin that filled with sediment from the adjacent arc
- C) An accretionary wedge — sediment and oceanic crustal material carried on the subducting plate were scraped off against the overriding plate and accreted as a compressed, chaotic wedge at the trench's inner wall ✓
- D) A suture zone — the rocks are the remnants of an ocean basin that was fully subducted, leaving only fragments of ocean floor preserved between two colliding continents

**Q3:** The Andes have a chain of active stratovolcanoes and an offshore trench. The Himalayas are higher but have no volcanic chain and no offshore trench. Both are orogenic belts at convergent boundaries. What is the fundamental difference in their tectonic setting?
- A) The Andes are older than the Himalayas; all subduction zones eventually lose their trench and volcanic arc as they mature into collision-type orogens like the Himalayas
- B) The Andes overlie an oceanic-continental subduction zone where the Nazca Plate releases water that triggers arc volcanism; the Himalayas formed when continental India collided with continental Asia — all intervening ocean was consumed, the continents met, neither could subduct, and the crust thickened into a fold-thrust belt with no subducting slab to generate arc magma ✓
- C) The difference is convergence rate: faster convergence (Andes) generates enough heat for volcanism; slower convergence (Himalayas) produces only mechanical thickening
- D) The Himalayas once had a volcanic arc and trench like the Andes, but both were eroded away by the intense precipitation on the southern flank of the range
