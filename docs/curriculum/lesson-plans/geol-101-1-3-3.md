## Learning Objectives

1. Distinguish the three sedimentary rock types (clastic, chemical, organic) by their formation process.
2. Identify common sedimentary rocks (sandstone, shale, conglomerate, limestone, rock salt, coal) by texture and composition.
3. Explain why sedimentary rocks are Earth's primary archive of surface history — fossils, ancient climates, and sea levels.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Built at the surface"

**Hook:** Stand at the South Rim of the Grand Canyon and look at the walls. Each horizontal band of colour is a different rock layer — a different chapter in Earth's surface history. The buff-coloured Coconino Sandstone near the top preserves ancient desert sand dunes from 275 million years ago. The red Hermit Shale below it records a coastal mudflat. The Redwall Limestone halfway down is a warm shallow sea from 340 million years ago. Every one of these layers is a sedimentary rock: material that settled out of water or wind at Earth's surface, accumulated, and was lithified into stone. The Grand Canyon is not just a canyon — it is 1.8 billion years of Earth history you can read like a book, one layer at a time.

**Key Terms:**
- **Sediment** — Loose particles of mineral or rock material (or organic material) that have been transported by water, wind, ice, or gravity and deposited at Earth's surface. Sediment becomes sedimentary rock through lithification.
- **Lithification** — The process by which loose sediment is converted into solid rock, primarily through compaction (pressure from overlying sediment reduces pore space) and cementation (minerals precipitate from groundwater to bind grains together).
- **Clastic sedimentary rock** — Rock formed from fragments (clasts) of pre-existing minerals and rocks that were weathered, transported, and deposited. Classified primarily by grain size. Examples: conglomerate, sandstone, siltstone, shale.
- **Chemical sedimentary rock** — Rock formed by precipitation of minerals directly from solution in water — either by evaporation, by changes in water chemistry, or by biological organisms extracting dissolved ions to build shells. Examples: limestone, rock salt, chert.
- **Organic sedimentary rock** — Rock formed primarily from accumulated organic material — the compressed remains of plants or organisms. Examples: coal (compressed plant material), some limestones (shell accumulations).

### Section 2 — Concept: "Three types, one archive"

**Body overview:**
- The four-step sequence: weathering → erosion → deposition → lithification
- Clastic rocks: grain size scale (clay→silt→sand→gravel/cobble/boulder), depositional energy, rock names
- Chemical rocks: limestone (marine carbonate), evaporites (rock salt, gypsum), chert (silica)
- Organic rocks: coal (terrestrial plant material), organic-rich shales
- Sedimentary structures as environment indicators: horizontal bedding (calm water), cross-bedding (currents/dunes), ripple marks (shallow moving water), graded bedding (turbidity currents)
- Fossils: why only sedimentary rocks preserve them; the stratigraphic record

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Loose sand grains on a beach are compacted by burial and cemented by minerals precipitating from groundwater. What is this process called, and what rock does it produce?
- A) Crystallisation — it produces an igneous rock because mineral growth from solution is the same process as magma cooling
- B) Lithification — compaction and cementation convert the loose sediment into sandstone, a clastic sedimentary rock  ✓
- C) Metamorphism — burial under pressure transforms loose sand into quartzite without any cementation step
- D) Diagenesis is only a chemical change and does not produce a true rock — the result remains a poorly consolidated sediment

**Q2:** A geologist finds two sedimentary rock layers deposited in the same ancient river system. Layer A is conglomerate with pebble-sized clasts. Layer B is shale with clay-sized particles. What does the grain size difference tell us about the depositional environments of each layer?
- A) Layer A was deposited closer to the source (high-energy fast water that could carry large clasts); Layer B was deposited in a low-energy setting such as a floodplain or lake where only fine particles could settle  ✓
- B) Layer A is older than Layer B because coarser grains always settle first during a single depositional event, regardless of energy
- C) Layer B was deposited closer to the source because fine clay particles are produced first by weathering before larger fragments break off
- D) Grain size in sedimentary rocks reflects the composition of the source rock, not the energy of the transport medium

**Q3:** A paleontologist finds a beautifully preserved trilobite fossil in a limestone layer. Why is the fossil found in sedimentary rock specifically, and not in an igneous or metamorphic rock of the same age?
- A) Fossils can form in all rock types, but geologists only look for them in sedimentary rocks by convention
- B) Igneous and metamorphic rocks are always too old to contain fossils — all life existed after these rock types stopped forming
- C) Sedimentary rocks form at low temperatures at Earth's surface, allowing organic remains to be buried and preserved; igneous rocks form from melt that would incinerate any organism, and metamorphic conditions of heat and pressure destroy organic structures  ✓
- D) Fossils are only found in limestone specifically, not in other sedimentary rock types like sandstone or shale
