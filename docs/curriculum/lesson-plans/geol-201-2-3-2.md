## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The clock in the rock"
**Hook:** In 1799, William Smith — a canal surveyor with no university education — published the observation that changed geology forever: each layer of rock contains a distinctive assemblage of fossils, and those assemblages always occur in the same vertical order. He used this observation to produce the first national geological map of Britain (1815), predicting, correctly, which rocks would underlie fields he had never visited. Smith's insight — the principle of faunal succession — established that fossils are not merely curiosities but clocks. Today, biostratigraphy uses that same principle to correlate strata on every continent and to build the numerical framework of the geological timescale. A single conodont extracted from a limestone chip can tell a petroleum geologist which interval of the Carboniferous they are drilling through. A planktonic foram from a sediment core off Newfoundland can be matched to the same species in a core from the South Pacific. This lesson explains how.

**Key terms:** biostratigraphy, faunal succession, index fossil, biozone, FAD (first appearance datum), LAD (last appearance datum), Signor–Lipps effect.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Index fossils and biozones"

#### The principle of faunal succession
- William Smith (1769–1839): "strata identified by organized fossils" — each layer contains a distinctive fauna; layers always in the same stratigraphic order
- The principle is empirical, not circular: the observation that taxon X always occurs below taxon Y is testable and has been confirmed globally
- **Biostratigraphy**: the branch of stratigraphy that uses fossil content to establish the relative age and correlation of rock units
- Biostratigraphy does not directly date rocks numerically; it establishes relative order; numerical ages come from radiometric dating of beds bracketing the biozone

#### Index fossils
- An ideal index fossil has four properties: (1) **wide geographic range** (global or near-global distribution, so correlation across continents is possible); (2) **short time range** (appears and disappears in a short stratigraphic interval, giving high time resolution); (3) **abundance** (easily found in sufficient numbers to be reliable); (4) **easy identification** (distinctive morphology that specialists and non-specialists can recognise); a fifth sometimes added is **facies independence** — occurs in multiple rock types, not restricted to one environment
- Most taxa have wide geographic range OR short time range, not both — the best zone fossils are exceptions that have both
- Planktonic and nektonic organisms (those that drifted or swam in the water column) tend to be better zone fossils than benthic organisms: they are distributed globally by ocean currents and are independent of local bottom conditions (facies)

#### Biozones
- **Biozone**: a body of rock defined or characterised by its fossil content; the fundamental unit of biostratigraphy
- Three types: (1) **Range zone** — defined by the total range of a single taxon from FAD to LAD; (2) **Interval zone** — the interval between the FAD of one taxon and the FAD (or LAD) of another; (3) **Assemblage zone** — defined by a characteristic association of multiple taxa; more robust than a single-taxon zone because partial preservation can still identify the zone
- **FAD (First Appearance Datum)**: the lowest stratigraphic horizon at which a taxon is recognised in a given section; approximates the evolution or immigration of that taxon, but may be younger than the true first occurrence due to incomplete sampling
- **LAD (Last Appearance Datum)**: the highest stratigraphic horizon; approximates extinction or emigration, but is always stratigraphically younger than (or equal to) the true last occurrence
- **Signor–Lipps effect** (1982): because sampling is incomplete, the observed FAD of any taxon will always be younger than the true evolutionary first occurrence, and the observed LAD will be older than the true extinction; the effect artificially smears out abrupt evolutionary events (including mass extinctions) in the raw fossil record

### Section 3 — Application: "Zone fossils through geologic time"

#### Key biostratigraphic tools by period
- **Trilobites** (Cambrian–Ordovician, ~538–443 Ma): highly diverse; many short-ranging species; key zone fossils for Cambrian and Lower Ordovician; global but benthic — facies dependent to a degree
- **Graptolites** (Ordovician–Silurian, ~485–418 Ma): colonial hemichordates; floated in the water column (planktonic to pseudoplanktonic); extremely short-ranging species (~0.5–2 Ma); global distribution in graptolitic shale facies; the principal tool for Ordovician–Silurian biostratigraphy worldwide; recognised by distinctive saw-tooth or spiral morphologies in black shale
- **Conodonts** (Cambrian–Late Triassic, ~510–201 Ma): microscopic phosphatic tooth-like feeding elements of an extinct jawless vertebrate; highly diverse, short-ranging species; global distribution in marine carbonates; the most biostratigraphically useful group in the Paleozoic–Triassic; recovered by dissolving carbonate rock in acid; primary tools for Ordovician, Devonian, Carboniferous, Permian, and Triassic zonation
- **Ammonites** (Devonian–Cretaceous, ~380–66 Ma): extinct cephalopods with coiled, sutured shells; short-ranging species (often <1 Ma); global distribution in marine sediments; the principal zone fossils for Jurassic and Cretaceous; suture patterns are distinctive and allow genus/species identification from fragmentary material; standard Mesozoic ammonite zones subdivide time to ~0.5 Ma resolution
- **Foraminifera** (Cambrian–present): single-celled marine protists with calcite or agglutinated tests; benthic forms useful regionally; **planktonic foraminifera** (evolved in the Cretaceous) are the primary biostratigraphic tool for Cretaceous–Cenozoic open-marine sediments; critical for oil and gas exploration (identify drill core position); calcareous nannofossils (coccoliths) are complementary tools in the same interval
- **Pollen and spores** (Silurian–present): useful in terrestrial and marginal marine settings where marine organisms are absent; primary tool for Carboniferous–Permian coal-bearing sequences and Cenozoic continental basins

#### Integration with other methods
- **Magnetostratigraphy**: Earth's magnetic field reverses on irregular timescales; the sequence of normal and reversed polarities recorded in rock is independent of fossil content; magnetostratigraphy provides a correlatable signal in rocks that lack suitable zone fossils (deep-sea carbonates, continental sediments)
- **Chemostratigraphy**: global carbon isotope excursions (δ¹³C), oxygen isotope ratios (δ¹⁸O), and strontium isotope ratios (⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr) record global ocean chemistry changes that are correlatable between sections; particularly important near mass extinction boundaries
- **Astrochronology**: orbital cycle records in sediment thickness and isotope ratios provide time resolution of ~20,000–400,000 years; used to calibrate the exact numerical age of biozone boundaries in the Cenozoic and Mesozoic
- **Integrated timescale**: the combination of biostratigraphy, magnetostratigraphy, chemostratigraphy, and radiometric dating produces the International Chronostratigraphic Chart — the community standard for assigning numerical ages to rock intervals

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. William Smith observed that each rock layer contains a distinctive fossil assemblage that always appears in the same vertical order. What principle does this observation describe, and why is it fundamental to biostratigraphy?
2. A fossil taxon has a global distribution in open-marine sediments, species with an average duration of approximately 1 million years, and a distinctive morphology easily identified from fragmentary material. Assess this taxon as a potential zone fossil and identify which properties make it suitable or unsuitable.
3. What is the Signor–Lipps effect, and what are its implications for interpreting the sharpness of mass extinction boundaries in the fossil record?
4. Explain why planktonic organisms (like graptolites and planktonic foraminifera) generally make better zone fossils than benthic organisms living on the seafloor.
5. A biostratigrapher wants to determine the precise age of a limestone from the Late Jurassic. She identifies an ammonite biozone (giving a relative age) and measures carbon isotope ratios in the carbonate. She also finds a volcanic ash layer she can date radiometrically. Describe how these three independent methods are combined to produce a calibrated age estimate.
