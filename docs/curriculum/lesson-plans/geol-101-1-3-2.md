## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "What cooling rate tells you"

**Hook:** Obsidian and granite can share nearly the same bulk chemical composition — both rich in silica, potassium, and sodium — yet obsidian is smooth, jet-black volcanic glass with no visible crystals, while granite has centimetre-scale crystals of feldspar and quartz you can see and touch. The only difference is how fast the melt cooled. Cooling rate is the master variable of igneous petrology: it controls crystal size, which controls texture, which is the first thing a geologist reads when identifying an igneous rock.

**Key Terms:**
- **Magma** — Molten rock beneath Earth's surface, including any dissolved gases and suspended crystals. When magma reaches the surface, it is called lava.
- **Lava** — Magma that has reached Earth's surface through a volcanic eruption. The term also applies to the rock formed when lava solidifies.
- **Intrusive (plutonic) igneous rock** — Igneous rock formed by slow cooling of magma underground, within the crust. Slow cooling allows large crystals to grow. Examples: granite, gabbro, diorite.
- **Extrusive (volcanic) igneous rock** — Igneous rock formed by rapid cooling of lava at or near Earth's surface. Rapid cooling produces small crystals or glass. Examples: basalt, rhyolite, obsidian.
- **Texture** — The size, shape, and arrangement of crystals (or glass) in a rock. In igneous rocks, texture is the primary indicator of cooling rate and therefore of the depth and setting in which the rock solidified.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Classifying igneous rocks"

**Body overview:**
- Intrusive vs extrusive: crystal size as a direct cooling-rate record
- Felsic vs mafic: silica content controls mineralogy, colour, and density
- The 2×2 igneous rock matrix: granite (felsic-intrusive), rhyolite (felsic-extrusive), gabbro (mafic-intrusive), basalt (mafic-extrusive)
- Special textures: porphyritic (two-stage cooling), vesicular (gas bubbles), glassy (obsidian), frothy (pumice)
- Where igneous rocks form: spreading centres (basalt), subduction zones (granite/andesite), hotspots (basalt) — brief, detail in Module 1.4

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Two igneous rock samples share the same chemical composition. One has crystals 2 cm across; the other has crystals too small to see without a magnifier. What accounts for the difference?
- A) The coarse-grained rock crystallised from a more silica-rich melt, which allows larger crystals to grow
- B) The coarse-grained rock cooled slowly underground, giving crystals time to grow large; the fine-grained rock cooled rapidly at or near the surface  ✓
- C) The fine-grained rock was metamorphosed after formation, which recrystallises minerals into smaller grains
- D) Grain size is controlled by the water content of the magma, not by cooling rate

**Q2:** A geologist describes a dark-coloured, fine-grained rock collected from the ocean floor. Which rock is it most likely to be, and what does "mafic" mean in terms of its composition?
- A) Granite — granite is the most common rock in the ocean floor because continents have eroded into the sea over billions of years
- B) Basalt — a mafic rock is low in silica and rich in magnesium and iron-bearing minerals such as pyroxene and olivine, producing a dark, dense rock  ✓
- C) Rhyolite — rhyolite is extrusive and fine-grained, which matches the ocean floor setting
- D) Obsidian — ocean floor rocks cool rapidly in seawater, always producing volcanic glass rather than crystalline rock

**Q3:** Pumice is an igneous rock so full of gas-bubble holes that it floats on water. Which two textural features does pumice display, and what do they record about how it formed?
- A) Porphyritic and intrusive — large phenocrysts surrounded by fine groundmass record two-stage underground cooling
- B) Glassy and vesicular — pumice solidified so quickly from gas-rich lava that it froze as frothy glass, preserving the bubble holes (vesicles) from dissolved volcanic gases  ✓
- C) Foliated and vesicular — the parallel fabric of pumice records directed pressure during metamorphism, while the holes record fluid escape
- D) Coarse-grained and vesicular — slow underground cooling produced large crystals, and gas bubbles were trapped before the rock fully solidified
