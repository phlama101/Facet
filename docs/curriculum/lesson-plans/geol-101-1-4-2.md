## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Where plates meet"

**Hook:** The San Andreas Fault runs 1,300 km through California. Along it, the Pacific Plate slides northward past the North American Plate at about 5 cm per year — meaning Los Angeles is slowly moving toward San Francisco. Yet the San Andreas produces no volcanoes, only earthquakes. Six thousand kilometres away, Iceland sits directly on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, is being slowly torn apart as the North American and Eurasian plates diverge, and erupts basalt so frequently that it has built an entire island. In the Himalayas, India is still colliding with Asia at 5 cm per year, pushing the mountains higher with no ocean trench and no volcanic chain in sight. Three boundaries, three completely different geological outcomes — because the type of motion at the boundary controls everything that happens there.

**Key Terms:**
- **Divergent boundary** — A plate boundary where two plates move apart. New oceanic crust is created by seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges. On continents, divergence produces rift valleys.
- **Convergent boundary** — A plate boundary where two plates move toward each other. One plate may subduct beneath the other, or (if both are continental) the plates collide and build mountain ranges.
- **Transform boundary** — A plate boundary where two plates slide horizontally past each other with no creation or destruction of crust. Motion is along strike-slip faults; produces earthquakes but no volcanoes.
- **Rift valley** — A linear depression formed where continental crust is being pulled apart at a divergent boundary. The East African Rift is an active example; the Atlantic Ocean began as a rift valley ~175 Ma.
- **Subduction zone** — A convergent boundary where one oceanic plate descends beneath another plate into the mantle. Marked by an ocean trench, a volcanic arc, and deep earthquakes along the descending slab.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Three boundaries, three outcomes"

**Body overview:**
- Divergent: seafloor spreading at mid-ocean ridges (basalt production, symmetric spreading, Iceland); continental rifting (East African Rift, Red Sea as young ocean)
- Convergent subtypes:
  - Oceanic-oceanic: denser/older plate subducts → deep ocean trench + island arc volcanism (Aleutians, Mariana Trench)
  - Oceanic-continental: oceanic subducts → trench + continental volcanic arc + metamorphic belt (Cascades, Andes, Cascadia)
  - Continental-continental: neither subducts easily → collision, no volcanism, fold-and-thrust mountain belt (Himalayas, Alps)
- Transform: lateral sliding, no crust created or destroyed, earthquakes only (San Andreas, Alpine Fault NZ, Dead Sea Transform)
- Connection to rock types: divergent → basalt; convergent → andesite/granite + metamorphic rocks; transform → no new igneous rock

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Iceland is one of the most volcanically active places on Earth and is slowly being split in two. Which boundary type explains this, and what process creates the new rock?
- A) Convergent — Iceland sits above a subduction zone where one plate descends beneath the other, generating magma that feeds the volcanoes
- B) Transform — the two plates sliding past each other generate friction heat that melts rock and produces volcanic eruptions
- C) Divergent — Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge where the North American and Eurasian plates are pulling apart; decompression melting of the rising mantle produces basalt that continuously builds new crust ✓
- D) Iceland's volcanism is unrelated to plate boundaries — it is entirely explained by a mantle hotspot beneath a stable plate interior

**Q2:** The Cascade Range (Washington, Oregon, northern California) contains active stratovolcanoes like Mt. St. Helens and Mt. Rainier. The Himalayas, by contrast, are the world's highest mountain range but contain no active volcanic chain. Both are convergent boundaries. What accounts for this difference?
- A) The Cascades are older than the Himalayas; older convergent boundaries always produce more volcanism than younger ones
- B) The Himalayas are too high — the mountains block the magma from reaching the surface, whereas the lower Cascades allow volcanic eruptions
- C) The Cascades overlie an oceanic-continental convergent zone where the oceanic Juan de Fuca Plate subducts beneath North America, releasing water that triggers melting and volcanism; the Himalayas formed from continental-continental collision where neither plate subducts significantly, so no water-triggered melting occurs ✓
- D) The difference is in spreading rate — fast-spreading boundaries (Cascades) produce volcanoes while slow-spreading boundaries (Himalayas) produce only mountains

**Q3:** The San Andreas Fault produces major earthquakes — including the 1906 San Francisco earthquake — but no volcanoes. Which boundary type is it, and why does this boundary produce earthquakes but no volcanic activity?
- A) Divergent — the plates are pulling apart along the fault, generating earthquakes as the crust stretches; the lack of volcanoes indicates the spreading rate is too slow to produce magma
- B) Convergent — one plate is slowly subducting beneath the other, but the subduction angle is so shallow that magma cannot reach the surface to form volcanoes
- C) Transform — the Pacific and North American plates slide horizontally past each other; no crust is created or destroyed so no magma is generated, but sudden release of accumulated stress along the fault produces large earthquakes ✓
- D) Transform — but the lack of volcanoes is temporary; as the plates continue to slide, eventual subduction will begin and volcanism will develop within a few million years
