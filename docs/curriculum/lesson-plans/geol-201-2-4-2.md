## Learning Objectives

1. Define mass extinction and distinguish it from background extinction in terms of rate and selectivity.
2. Describe the "Big Five" mass extinctions, their timing, severity, and primary victims.
3. Explain the mechanism of the end-Permian extinction via Siberian Traps volcanism and its cascading environmental effects.
4. Explain the cause and mechanism of the end-Cretaceous extinction via the Chicxulub bolide impact.
5. Describe extinction selectivity and how mass extinctions reset evolutionary trajectories by eliminating dominant groups and opening ecological space.

## Scope Boundaries

**Covers:** Background vs. mass extinction; the Big Five (end-Ordovician, Late Devonian, end-Permian, end-Triassic, end-Cretaceous); mechanisms of end-Permian (LIP volcanism) and K-Pg (bolide + Deccan volcanism); extinction selectivity; disaster faunas; evolutionary aftermath.
**Does not cover:** The current (sixth) mass extinction in detail, individual group-level phylogenetics, detailed geochemistry of extinction signatures beyond overview.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Five great dyings"
**Hook:** At 252 Ma, 96% of marine species and 70% of terrestrial vertebrate species went extinct. Reefs — which had existed continuously for 400 million years — collapsed entirely and did not return for 10 million years. On land, a single genus of mammal-like reptile (*Lystrosaurus*) came to represent 95% of all terrestrial vertebrate individuals across much of the world. Recovery from the end-Permian extinction took between 5 and 10 million years. It was not the only mass extinction in Earth history — there were at least four others significant enough to reset the trajectory of life — and the mechanism that drove the worst of them was not an asteroid but volcanoes.

**Key terms:** background extinction, mass extinction, the Big Five, Large Igneous Province (LIP), bolide impact, Chicxulub, K-Pg boundary, disaster fauna, ecological release.

### Section 2 — Concept: "The Big Five — patterns and causes"

#### Background vs. mass extinction
- **Background extinction**: the continuous, low-level extinction of species throughout geological time; average species lifetime ~1–10 Ma; under background conditions, ~0.1–1 species go extinct per million species per year
- **Mass extinction**: a global event in which ≥75% of species go extinct in a geologically brief interval, typically <1–2 Ma (and often much shorter); characterised by non-selectivity across ecological guilds and geographic regions; five events qualify as mass extinctions in the Phanerozoic record (Raup & Sepkoski, 1982)
- The "Big Five" in order of geological age:

#### End-Ordovician (~443 Ma) — ~85% of species
- A two-pulse event driven by the glaciation of Gondwana (the supercontinent then at the south pole): the first pulse was caused by rapid cooling and sea level fall as ice sheets built up, eliminating shallow marine habitats; the second pulse occurred during rapid warming and deglaciation, which caused ocean stratification, anoxia, and carbonate chemistry disruption
- Primary victims: graptolites (>50% of genera eliminated), trilobites (began their long Paleozoic decline), brachiopods, bryozoans, echinoderms; deep-water fauna less affected than shallow shelf
- Recovery was relatively rapid (~2 Ma) because the causes were physical/climatic rather than chemical

#### Late Devonian (~375–359 Ma) — ~75% of species
- A prolonged, multi-pulse event spanning ~15–20 Ma; the Frasnian–Famennian (F–F) boundary (~372 Ma) is the most acute crisis; multiple lesser events before and after
- Collapse of the Devonian reef ecosystem: the spectacular Late Devonian reef builders — stromatoporoids, tabulate and rugose corals — were essentially eliminated and never recovered; reef ecosystems did not fully recover until the Triassic (~252–251 Ma)
- Primary victims: placoderm fish (armoured jawed fish — the dominant vertebrates of the Devonian — went extinct entirely), reef-building organisms, many marine invertebrate groups; relatively little terrestrial impact
- Cause: debated; candidates include global cooling from land plant expansion drawing down CO₂, marine anoxia events (black shales deposited globally), sea level change, possible bolide impacts (several spherule layers identified), volcanic activity; no consensus

#### End-Permian (~252 Ma) — ~96% of marine species
- The "Great Dying" — the most severe extinction in animal history; trilobites (which had survived 4 previous mass extinctions) went entirely extinct; 57% of all biological families, 83% of all genera eliminated; coal gap lasts ~10 Ma afterward
- Cause: **Siberian Traps Large Igneous Province** — erupted ~3 million km³ of basaltic lava over ~60,000 years in what is now Siberia; the eruption was catastrophic in its environmental effects: CO₂ release caused +8–10°C warming; sulfur dioxide caused acid rain; chlorine and bromine compounds destroyed stratospheric ozone; intrusion of magma into Permian coal and carbonate deposits released additional CO₂ and CH₄; ocean acidification from CO₂ absorption; anoxic dead zones in oceans; the combination was synergistic — no single effect alone explains the severity
- Cards: The Big Five — Timing and Severity (Layers, BRAND.gold), End-Permian: the Great Dying (Mountain, BRAND.coral)

### Section 3 — Application: "The K-Pg boundary and extinction aftermath"

#### End-Triassic (~201 Ma) — ~80% of species
- Coincident with the Central Atlantic Magmatic Province (CAMP) — the LIP associated with the opening of the Atlantic Ocean as Pangaea began rifting; same mechanism as end-Permian (volcanic CO₂ and acidification) but less severe
- Primary victims: marine reptiles (ichthyosaurs reduced), many marine invertebrate groups, large amphibians and non-dinosaurian archosaurs on land
- Ecological consequence: the End-Triassic extinction eliminated the dominant land animals (non-dinosaurian archosaurs), opening ecological space that allowed dinosaurs to diversify into the dominant terrestrial vertebrates of the Jurassic

#### End-Cretaceous / K-Pg (~66.043 Ma) — ~76% of species
- The best-studied mass extinction; evidence for cause is unambiguous
- **Chicxulub impactor**: a ~10–12 km diameter asteroid or comet impacted the Yucatán Peninsula (Mexico) at ~20 km/s, releasing energy equivalent to ~1 billion Hiroshima-scale nuclear bombs; the impact vaporised carbonate and sulfate target rocks, releasing CO₂ and SO₃; the crater is 180 km in diameter, buried beneath the Gulf of Mexico
- Evidence: global iridium anomaly at the K-Pg boundary (iridium is rare on Earth's surface but common in chondritic meteorites — Alvarez et al., 1980); shocked quartz (mineral grains with planar deformation features from shock pressures >10 GPa) globally distributed; Chicxulub crater (identified 1991); tektite and spherule layers
- Mechanisms: (1) **impact winter** — sulfate aerosols and dust ejected into stratosphere blocked sunlight globally for months; photosynthesis collapsed; food web collapse from base; (2) acid rain from sulfuric and nitric acid in atmosphere; (3) re-entry of ejecta globally caused brief but intense thermal pulse (infrared radiation from re-entering ejecta heated the atmosphere); (4) long-term cooling (sulfate aerosols) then warming (CO₂ from impact + Deccan volcanism); the Deccan Traps LIP (India) was also erupting at 66 Ma and may have contributed independent environmental stress
- Primary victims: non-avian dinosaurs, marine and flying reptiles (mosasaurs, plesiosaurs, pterosaurs), ammonites (completely eliminated), many marine plankton groups (calcareous nannoplankton severely reduced); approximately 75% of species lost
- Survivors: birds (avian dinosaurs survived — probably due to small body size, dietary flexibility, and ability to exploit disturbed post-impact ecosystems); mammals (similarly advantaged by small size, burrowing, and omnivory); crocodilians, turtles, lizards, snakes; most freshwater fauna; some marine invertebrate groups

#### Extinction selectivity and aftermath
- Mass extinctions are not random — certain traits confer survival advantage: wide geographic distribution (cosmopolitan vs. endemic), dietary generalism (omnivore vs. specialist), small body size (less food required), burrowing behavior (shelter from thermal pulse and food scarcity)
- **Ecological release**: when dominant groups are eliminated, previously subordinate or marginal lineages diversify rapidly into vacant ecological niches; after K-Pg, mammals diversified from small, generalist insectivores into every ecological role vacated by non-avian dinosaurs within ~10 Ma
- **Disaster faunas**: immediately after a mass extinction, low-diversity assemblages dominated by a few opportunistic, stress-tolerant species colonise devastated environments; after the end-Permian, *Lystrosaurus* (a mammal-like reptile) comprised ~95% of terrestrial vertebrate individuals in some areas; after K-Pg, fern spore spikes record the pioneer vegetation recolonising devastated landscapes
- **Recovery times**: end-Permian recovery required ~5–10 Ma for full ecosystem complexity to be re-established; K-Pg recovery was faster (~5 Ma) because many groups with ecological roles survived; recovery is never a return to the pre-extinction state — the post-extinction fauna is always different in composition
- Card: Selectivity, Aftermath, and Ecological Release (Clock, BRAND.amethyst)

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. Define mass extinction and explain how it differs from background extinction in terms of rate, selectivity, and ecological impact.
2. The end-Permian extinction (~252 Ma) is the most severe extinction in animal history. Describe the primary cause (Siberian Traps volcanism) and explain at least three distinct environmental mechanisms that cascaded from it to drive such catastrophic biodiversity loss.
3. What physical and chemical evidence at the K-Pg boundary (~66 Ma) demonstrates that a large bolide impacted Earth at that time, and how does each line of evidence constrain the nature and size of the impactor?
4. Non-avian dinosaurs went extinct at the K-Pg boundary, but birds (avian dinosaurs) survived. What traits or circumstances likely allowed birds to survive the impact winter and subsequent ecological collapse?
5. Explain the concept of "ecological release" following a mass extinction. Use the K-Pg extinction and the subsequent Paleocene–Eocene mammalian radiation as an example.
