## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The pages torn from the book"
**Hook:** Imagine a book with pages torn out. The story jumps from chapter 3 to chapter 7 with no warning, no explanation, just a ragged edge where the missing pages once were. Sedimentary rock sequences have exactly this problem. Across the world, in canyon walls and sea cliffs and road cuts, there are surfaces where millions — sometimes hundreds of millions — of years of geological history are simply absent. James Hutton recognised one of the most famous of these surfaces at Siccar Point, Scotland, in 1788: tilted Silurian greywackes, eroded flat, buried under horizontal Devonian sandstone. He understood, perhaps for the first time anyone did, that the gap between those two rock bodies represented an unimaginably vast interval of time — time that had left no record in the rocks.

**Key terms:** unconformity, disconformity, angular unconformity, nonconformity, erosion surface, hiatus, Siccar Point, Great Unconformity.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Gaps in the record"

#### What is an unconformity?
- An **unconformity** is a surface of erosion or non-deposition that separates younger strata above from older strata below, representing a gap in the geological record — a **hiatus** — during which sediment was removed, not deposited, or both
- Unconformities break the principle of superposition in its naive application: the rocks below may not simply be "older by a fixed amount" than the rocks above; the gap could be a few thousand years or several hundred million years
- Unconformities break Walther's Law: the facies above and below an unconformity were NOT necessarily deposited in laterally adjacent environments; the erosion surface severed the original environmental continuity
- Recognition in outcrop: (1) truncation of beds below the surface; (2) weathered or chemically altered horizon at the contact; (3) conglomerate or lag deposit at the base of the overlying unit (reworked material from the erosion); (4) abrupt change in lithology, fossil content, or metamorphic grade; (5) angular discordance of bedding above and below

#### Three types of unconformity
- **Disconformity:** the beds above and below are parallel (no angular discordance), but there is clear evidence of erosion between them — a wavy or irregular erosion surface, a basal conglomerate, a weathering profile, an abrupt change in fossil assemblage; implies uplift and erosion without significant tilting; common in cratonic settings
- **Angular unconformity:** the beds below dip at a different angle from (i.e., are truncated against) the beds above; this geometry requires that the lower sequence was deposited, tilted or folded by tectonics, eroded flat, and then submerged for new deposition; classic example: Siccar Point, Scotland — sub-vertical Silurian greywackes beneath gently dipping Devonian red sandstone; implies a complete orogenic cycle (deposition → mountain building → erosion → renewed basin formation) in the gap
- **Nonconformity:** sedimentary rocks rest directly on igneous or metamorphic basement (plutonic or high-grade metamorphic rocks); the basement was exhumed by deep erosion before burial; implies extreme erosion removing the entire overlying sedimentary cover plus some basement; major nonconformities often represent very large time gaps (hundreds of millions of years); example: sedimentary rocks resting on Precambrian granite in many North American cratonic sections

#### Formation: the unconformity cycle
1. **Deposition:** sediment accumulates in a basin
2. **Uplift:** tectonic forces raise the region above sea level (or above base level in continental settings)
3. **Erosion:** the exposed rocks are weathered and eroded; the duration of this step controls how much time is missing — a brief emergence may remove only a few thousand years of record; prolonged erosion removes millions of years
4. **Subsidence and renewed deposition:** the region sinks back below base level; new sediment is deposited on the erosion surface; the unconformity is "sealed" and preserved

#### The Great Unconformity
- One of the most significant stratigraphic surfaces in North America and possibly globally: in the Grand Canyon, Cambrian sandstone (Tapeats Sandstone, ~505 Ma) rests directly on Precambrian crystalline basement (~1,740 Ma) or on tilted Precambrian sedimentary rocks (Grand Canyon Supergroup, 740–1,250 Ma)
- The gap represented by this surface is ~1,200 Ma in some locations (where it separates 505 Ma from 1,740 Ma basement) — nearly 1.2 billion years of geological history is missing
- The cause of the Great Unconformity is debated; most likely involves the Snowball Earth glaciations (~720–635 Ma) and the breakup of the Rodinia supercontinent creating extensive erosion surfaces

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading unconformities in the field"

#### Recognising unconformities
- Field checklist: (1) angular discordance of bedding → angular unconformity; (2) parallel beds but with basal conglomerate/lag/weathering profile → disconformity; (3) sedimentary rock directly on intrusive/metamorphic rock → nonconformity; (4) abrupt lithological change with no gradational transition; (5) dramatic change in fossil assemblage across a sharp surface; (6) palaeosol (fossil soil) horizon — indicates prolonged exposure

#### Quantifying the hiatus
- The duration of the gap cannot be measured directly from the unconformity surface itself — you need radiometric dates from rocks on both sides (or from within the erosion surface if datable material is present)
- Example: if the rocks immediately below an unconformity are dated at 400 Ma and those immediately above at 350 Ma, the hiatus is at least 50 Myr (and possibly more, if the topmost pre-unconformity rocks were removed by erosion)
- The gap may be extremely variable laterally: the same unconformity may represent 10 Myr in one location and 100 Myr elsewhere depending on how much erosion occurred

#### Stratigraphic significance
- Unconformities divide rock sequences into packages that can be correlated regionally and globally — sequence stratigraphy (2.2.3) formalises this
- Major unconformities often correspond to plate tectonic events: continent-continent collision (produces angular unconformities as mountain belts rise and erode), global sea-level falls (expose continental shelves for erosion), glaciations (both erosion by glaciers and sea-level fall during glacial maxima)
- In petroleum geology, unconformities are important because the erosion surface often juxtaposes porous reservoir rock against impermeable cap rock, creating traps; the unconformity itself may be a reservoir surface
