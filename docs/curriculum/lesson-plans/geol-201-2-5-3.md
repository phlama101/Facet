## Learning Objectives

1. Describe the purpose and procedure of measuring a stratigraphic section using a Jacob's staff and compass clinometer.
2. Classify sedimentary grain sizes using the Wentworth scale and explain the significance of grain size for interpreting depositional energy.
3. Use Munsell notation to describe rock and sediment colour, and explain why standardised colour notation matters in geological description.
4. Identify and interpret the six most common sedimentary structures — cross-bedding, graded bedding, ripple marks, mud cracks, bioturbation, and flute casts — in terms of the depositional process that produced each.
5. Construct a graphic log of a measured stratigraphic section using standard lithology symbols and column notation.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The section is the data"
**Hook:** Before any geological interpretation can be made, someone has to measure and describe the rock. A measured stratigraphic section — a systematic, metre-by-metre record of every bed, its thickness, its grain size, its colour, its sedimentary structures, and its contacts — is the primary data product of sedimentary fieldwork. Everything else — palaeoenvironmental reconstruction, sequence stratigraphic analysis, biostratigraphic correlation — depends on having an accurate, complete, reproducible physical description of what is actually there. This lesson teaches how to produce that description: from choosing a section locality to completing a graphic column.

**Key terms:** measured section, Jacob's staff, Wentworth scale, Munsell colour, sedimentary structure, graphic log, lithofacies, contact, marker bed, stratigraphic column.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Measuring and describing"

#### Measuring a stratigraphic section
- **Purpose**: to produce a metre-by-metre record of bed thickness, lithology, grain size, colour, structures, contacts, and fossils — a quantitative and reproducible description usable by any geologist.
- **Jacob's staff**: a 1.5 m (or 1.0 m) rod with a clinometer fixed at one end. For dipping beds, the staff is held at the measured dip angle so that the top of the staff represents the same stratigraphic horizon as the base; this eliminates the trigonometric correction for apparent thicknesses on slopes. Reading: one staff length = 1.5 m of true stratigraphic thickness (if dip is set correctly).
- **Pace-and-compass** (flat-lying sections): for gently dipping or flat-lying exposures a tape measure along the section line with GPS georeferencing suffices.
- **Field notebook columns**: standard recording format includes — (from left to right) cumulative thickness, bed number, lithology symbol column, grain-size column, colour, sedimentary structures, contacts (coded), fossils, notes.
- **Contacts**: *sharp* (boundary defined within <1 cm); *gradational* (boundary diffuse over several cm to >1 m, beds merge gradually); *erosive* (lower bed is incised by the upper — indicates a scour surface); *conformable* (no time gap, beds parallel); *unconformable* (time gap, beds may be discordant).
- **Marker beds**: distinctive, laterally continuous beds (bentonite ash layers, distinctive limestones, coal seams) used as correlation datums between sections.

#### Grain size — the Wentworth scale
- **Clay**: <0.004 mm (4 μm); forms mudstone/shale when lithified; deposited from suspension in very low-energy environments.
- **Silt**: 0.004–0.063 mm; forms siltstone; deposited in low-energy environments (distal fluvial floodplains, prodelta, deep marine).
- **Sand**: 0.063–2 mm; forms sandstone; subdivided as very fine/fine/medium/coarse/very coarse; deposited in moderate-energy environments (rivers, beaches, aeolian dunes, turbidite currents).
- **Granule**: 2–4 mm; Pebble: 4–64 mm; Cobble: 64–256 mm; Boulder: >256 mm — collectively *gravel*, forms conglomerate; deposited in high-energy environments (braided rivers, alluvial fans, beaches, glacial outwash).
- **Field determination**: use a grain-size card (a card with calibrated grids printed on it) held against the rock in raking light; sand grains are visible and roughly gritty to touch; silt feels slightly gritty; clay feels smooth and plastic when wet.

#### Munsell colour notation
- **Why standardise colour?**: colour perception varies between observers and under different lighting conditions. Two geologists describing "yellowish-brown" may mean different things. Munsell provides an objective notation.
- **Three parameters**: *Hue* (the spectral colour, e.g., 10YR = yellow-red at the yellow end; 5R = red); *Value* (lightness, 0 = black, 10 = white); *Chroma* (colour saturation, 0 = grey, /8 = vivid). Written as: Hue Value/Chroma (e.g., 10YR 6/4 = light yellowish-brown — a common sandstone colour).
- **Using the Munsell book**: compare a moist chip of the rock to the Munsell chips under consistent (preferably natural daylight) lighting. Separate notation for wet and dry if significant difference exists.
- **Most common geological hues**: 10YR–2.5Y for sandstones and limestones; 5YR–7.5YR for red beds; 5GY–5G for reduced (grey-green) shales; N (neutral grey) for dark shales and coal.

### Section 3 — Application: "Sedimentary structures and facies interpretation"

#### Common sedimentary structures and their significance
- **Cross-bedding** (cross-stratification): inclined laminae within a bed, dipping in the direction of current flow; the orientation of foresets indicates palaeocurrent direction; the scale of cross-bedding relates to the size of the bedform (ripples → small-scale; dunes → large-scale, up to metres). Environments: rivers, beaches, aeolian dunes, tidal channels, turbidites.
- **Graded bedding**: systematic decrease in grain size from base (coarser) to top (finer) within a single bed; produced by turbidity currents (dense, sediment-laden flows that sort grains as they decelerate). The **Bouma sequence** (Ta-Tb-Tc-Td-Te) describes the full suite of structures in a turbidite bed.
- **Ripple marks**: small, regular bedforms (wavelength < 10 cm); *current ripples* are asymmetric (steep downstream lee face); *wave ripples* are symmetric; preserved ripple crests indicate current direction or wave oscillation direction.
- **Mud cracks (desiccation cracks)**: polygonal fracture patterns in fine-grained sediment formed when it dries out and contracts; indicates periodic subaerial exposure (intertidal flats, floodplains, playa lakes).
- **Bioturbation**: disruption of primary sedimentary structures by organism burrowing or burrowing activity; high bioturbation index (BI 4–5) indicates slow, steady, low-energy deposition with abundant benthic life; absent bioturbation (BI 0) in rapid deposition or anoxic conditions (turbidites, black shales).
- **Flute casts**: bulbous, asymmetric scour marks on the bases of sandstone beds, formed by turbulent eddies at the leading edge of a turbidity current; point up-current (useful palaeocurrent indicators in deep-marine sequences).

#### Constructing a graphic column
- **Standard symbols**: shale = horizontal lines; siltstone = dashes; sandstone = dots (coarser = larger dots); limestone = brick pattern; conglomerate = circles/ovals; coal = black infill.
- **Width of column**: some conventions use a fixed-width column (all lithologies same width); others use a fining-upward or coarsening-upward column where the width represents grain size (wider = coarser). The latter (grain-size column) is more informative for environmental interpretation.
- **Facies sequences**: fining-upward cycles (coarse base grading to mud top) = fluvial channel abandonment or decelerating turbidite; coarsening-upward cycles (mud base to sand top) = delta progradation or shallowing-upward shallow marine.

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. What is the purpose of a Jacob's staff in measuring a stratigraphic section, and how does it account for the dip of the beds being measured?
2. A geologist describes a sandstone bed as follows: "Well-sorted, medium-grained (0.3–0.5 mm) sandstone with large-scale planar cross-beds (dipping 25° to the NNE, foreset height 40 cm), sharp erosive base, gradational top into overbank siltstone." Using the Wentworth scale and the significance of the sedimentary structures, what depositional environment does this description suggest?
3. What does Munsell notation 5YR 5/6 tell you about a rock sample's colour? Why is standardised colour notation preferable to descriptive terms like "reddish-brown" in geological field records?
4. Describe the formation mechanism of graded bedding and explain what it tells you about the depositional process. In what environments would you expect to find well-developed graded beds, and why are they useful for determining stratigraphic way-up (younging direction)?
5. You observe a 20 m section of alternating sandstones and mudstones. Each sandstone bed has a sharp, erosive base, internal cross-bedding, and a gradational top into the overlying mudstone. The mudstones show desiccation cracks and root traces. Construct a brief verbal description of the depositional environment and the likely sedimentary facies model that explains this repetitive pattern.
