## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Ice, mammals, and the making of the modern world"
**Hook:** Sixty-six million years ago, the world had no polar ice. Palm trees grew in the Arctic. Crocodilians inhabited the high latitudes of Canada. The global average temperature was 10–12°C warmer than today. The dominant land animals were non-avian dinosaurs. Then a bolide struck, and within a few million years of that catastrophic moment, the planet's fauna was unrecognisable — diverse mammals, birds, and modern-style reptiles filling every ecological role the dinosaurs had vacated. Over the following 66 million years, Earth gradually cooled from a "greenhouse" world to the glaciated world we inhabit today, with ice sheets covering Antarctica and cycling over North America and Europe on 100,000-year rhythms. The Cenozoic is not ancient history. The world it produced — its mammals, its climates, its geography — is the world we live in.

**Key terms:** Cenozoic, PETM (Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum), Eocene–Oligocene transition, Drake Passage, Milankovitch cycles, glacial–interglacial cycle, mid-Pleistocene transition, placental mammal, Great American Biotic Interchange (GABI), megafauna extinction, overkill hypothesis.

### Section 2 — Concept: "A planet in long-term cooling"

#### Cenozoic climate evolution
- **Paleocene (~66–56 Ma)**: warm, humid, no polar ice; rapid recovery and diversification of mammals, birds, and other post-K-Pg survivors; by end-Paleocene, mammals already diverse in form
- **Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum (PETM, ~56 Ma)**: one of the most rapid natural climate change events in the geological record; global temperature spiked +5–8°C above already-warm Eocene baseline within ~20,000 years; likely caused by a massive release of carbon (CO₂ and CH₄) from volcanic activity associated with the opening of the North Atlantic, combined with possible seafloor methane clathrate destabilisation; ocean acidification; carbonate dissolution horizon deepened in all oceans; deep-sea foraminifera (benthic) experienced massive extinction; recovery ~200,000 years; the PETM is a key analogue for current anthropogenic warming (rates are comparable, though modern carbon release is faster)
- **Early to Middle Eocene (~56–38 Ma)**: the warmest sustained interval of the Cenozoic; fully ice-free poles; no permanent ice anywhere on Earth; "hothouse" conditions; first modern mammal orders appearing and diversifying
- **Eocene–Oligocene transition (~34 Ma)**: the most abrupt climate step in the Cenozoic; global temperature fell ~5°C within ~400,000 years; the **Antarctic Ice Sheet** formed for the first time in at least 34 Ma; caused by: (1) opening of the **Drake Passage** (between South America and Antarctica, ~34–30 Ma) allowing the **Antarctic Circumpolar Current** to thermally isolate Antarctica from warm subtropical waters; (2) declining atmospheric CO₂ below a threshold (~750 ppm) that could no longer maintain ice-free conditions at the poles; direct result: sea level fell ~70 m; major extinction of marine plankton and some terrestrial groups; restructuring of global ocean circulation
- **Miocene (~23–5 Ma)**: moderate temperatures; Antarctic ice sheet waxed and waned; expansion of global grassland biomes from ~20 Ma, with C4 grasses (using the more efficient C4 photosynthetic pathway) expanding dramatically from ~7 Ma; horse teeth elongated and became high-crowned (hypsodont) to process tough silica-rich grasses; hominoid primates diversified in Africa
- **Pliocene (~5–2.6 Ma)**: further cooling; closure of the **Isthmus of Panama** (~3.5–3 Ma) changed ocean circulation, redirecting warm Atlantic water northward and altering precipitation patterns; Northern Hemisphere glaciation began ~2.6 Ma
- **Pleistocene (~2.6 Ma–11.7 ka)**: cyclical glaciations with ice sheets over North America, northern Europe, and Scandinavia; sea level fluctuated by ~120 m between glacial maxima and interglacials

#### Milankovitch orbital cycles and glacial–interglacial rhythms
- **Eccentricity** (100,000-year cycle): variation in the ellipticity of Earth's orbit around the Sun; controls the total amount of solar energy received over the year
- **Obliquity** (41,000-year cycle): variation in the tilt of Earth's axis (21.5°–24.5°); controls the seasonal contrast between summer and winter; high obliquity = more seasonal extremes; primary driver of high-latitude insolation
- **Precession** (23,000-year cycle): wobble of Earth's rotational axis; controls which hemisphere experiences summer at perihelion (closest approach to Sun); currently the Northern Hemisphere experiences summer near aphelion (farthest point), reducing summer insolation
- **Early Pleistocene (2.6–0.8 Ma)**: glacial cycles occurred on a 41,000-year rhythm, dominated by obliquity; ice sheets grew and shrank relatively symmetrically
- **Mid-Pleistocene transition (~1.2–0.7 Ma)**: glacial cycles shifted from 41 ka to 100 ka rhythms without any obvious change in orbital forcing; glacials became longer, colder, and more severe; the cause is debated — leading hypotheses involve declining atmospheric CO₂ or removal of regolith (soft sediment) beneath ice sheets exposing hard bedrock, allowing larger ice sheets to build
- **Last Glacial Maximum (~21 ka)**: ice sheets reached maximum extent; Laurentide Ice Sheet over North America up to 3–4 km thick; Fennoscandian Ice Sheet over northern Europe; global sea level ~120 m below present; Britain connected to Europe; Beringia (the Bering land bridge) connected Asia to North America

### Section 3 — Application: "Mammals inherit the Earth"

#### Mammalian adaptive radiation after K-Pg
- Living mammals comprise three groups: **monotremes** (egg-laying: platypus, echidna — ~5 species, restricted to Australia and New Guinea); **marsupials** (pouched young: kangaroos, koalas, possums, opossums — ~330 species, primarily Australia and South America); **placental mammals** (embryo nourished via placenta until advanced development — the dominant group with >5,500 species)
- Immediately after K-Pg (~66–60 Ma): small, generalist, insectivore-like placentals dominate; earliest representatives of many modern orders are tiny, shrew-like creatures
- **Eocene rapid diversification** (~56–34 Ma): major mammalian orders diverged rapidly: **Perissodactyla** (horses, rhinos, tapirs — odd-toed ungulates); **Artiodactyla** (pigs, cattle, deer, camels, hippos — even-toed ungulates); **Cetacea** (whales — evolved from terrestrial artiodactyl-like ancestors, with the full aquatic transition documented from Pakicetus (~53 Ma, walking) to Ambulocetus (~49 Ma, amphibious) to Basilosaurus (~40 Ma, fully aquatic)); **Chiroptera** (bats); **Rodentia** (rats, mice, squirrels — the most species-rich mammalian order); **Primates** (lemurs, monkeys, apes)
- **Oligocene–Miocene** (~34–5 Ma): diversification of ruminants (cows, deer, giraffes); expansion of grass-eating lineages; horses evolve from small browsing Eohippus to large grazing Equus over 54 Ma of continuous evolution in North America (the best-documented example of anagenetic evolution in the fossil record); whales diversify into baleen and toothed forms; hominoid apes diversify in Africa (~25–15 Ma)

#### Great American Biotic Interchange (GABI, ~3 Ma)
- North and South America were isolated from each other for ~30 Ma (from ~35 Ma when the Panamanian connection was broken); South American fauna evolved in isolation: marsupial carnivores (sparassodonts), xenarthrans (armadillos, anteaters, ground sloths), meridiungulates (South American ungulates with no northern relatives); North America had placental mammals including horses, gomphotheres (elephants), camels, deer, and large carnivores
- Isthmus of Panama closed at ~3.5–3 Ma: land bridge reconnected North and South America
- North American invaders of South America: horses (*Equus*), tapirs, deer, gomphotheres (elephants), pumas, jaguars, spectacled bears — largely successful; most North American immigrants persisted
- South American invaders of North America: ground sloths (*Megatherium* — the size of an elephant), glyptodonts (armoured armadillo relatives the size of a car), opossums (*Didelphis*), capybaras, armadillos — mixed success; ground sloths and glyptodonts went extinct at ~12 ka but were widespread; opossums, armadillos, and capybara still present today
- Result: most South American endemic groups went extinct or declined sharply (sparassodonts, most meridiungulates); North American fauna dominated; this demonstrates competitive asymmetry — placental mammals were ecologically superior to most South American marsupial and endemic placental groups in direct competition

#### Pleistocene megafauna extinction (~50,000–10,000 years ago)
- Large animals (>44 kg body mass) went extinct globally but not simultaneously: Australia ~46–40 ka (diprotodon, giant kangaroos, Thylacoleo); North America ~13–10 ka (woolly mammoths, mastodons, ground sloths, glyptodonts, American horses, short-faced bears, sabretooths); Europe ~14–10 ka (woolly mammoths, woolly rhinoceros, Irish elk/Megaloceros, cave lions, cave bears)
- Africa relatively spared: African large mammals had co-evolved with human ancestors for millions of years and were less naive to human hunting
- The debate: **climate hypothesis** — glacial–interglacial climate shifts altered habitats and food availability; **overkill hypothesis** (Paul Martin, 1966) — human hunters arriving on naïve continents drove rapid extinctions; **synergistic hypothesis** — climate reduced populations; humans delivered the final blow
- Evidence for human cause: extinctions correlate with human arrival (Polynesia → megafaunal extinctions follow settlement by 1–2 ka; Americas extinction timing matches Clovis hunter appearance ~13 ka); animals on continents long occupied by humans (Africa, southern Asia) survived much better than those in regions humans colonised late
- Evidence for climate: glacial–interglacial transitions had occurred multiple times before with limited extinctions; some extinctions pre-date human arrival in some regions; woolly rhinoceros may have been declining before humans arrived
- Current consensus: human hunting was the primary driver in the Americas and Australia (where animals had no evolutionary experience with human hunters); climate and humans acted synergistically in Europe; Africa's long co-evolution with hominids provided partial "training"

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. Describe two major tectonic events and one atmospheric change that drove the long-term Cenozoic cooling trend from the Eocene greenhouse to Pleistocene glaciation.
2. Explain the three principal Milankovitch orbital cycles and describe the mid-Pleistocene transition: what changed in the glacial rhythm and what is the significance of this transition?
3. Describe the evolution of whales from terrestrial ancestors. What fossil evidence documents this transition, and which major morphological changes occurred?
4. What was the Great American Biotic Interchange (~3 Ma), and why did North American mammals generally succeed in South America better than South American mammals succeeded in North America?
5. Evaluate the climate and overkill hypotheses for the Pleistocene megafauna extinction. Which lines of evidence most strongly support each hypothesis, and what does the geographic pattern of extinctions (Africa largely spared; Americas and Australia most severely affected) tell us about the relative importance of each cause?
