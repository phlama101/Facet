## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The geologist's field notebook"
**Hook:** A stratigraphic column is the most fundamental document in sedimentary geology. It is a scaled, annotated vertical diagram of a rock sequence — a field notebook entry that condenses hours or days of outcrop measurement into a single graphic that can be read by any geologist, anywhere, instantly. From a good stratigraphic column you can reconstruct the history of an ancient landscape: which way the rivers flowed, how deep the sea was, when a shoreline advanced or retreated, where a volcanic eruption dropped ash. Every professional geologist can draw one and read one. This lesson is about how.

**Key terms:** stratigraphic column (measured section), lithological symbol, grain-size curve, contact, fining-upward sequence, coarsening-upward sequence, sedimentary structure, correlation.

### Section 2 — Concept: "The anatomy of a stratigraphic column"

#### Structure and conventions
- A stratigraphic column plots depth/height on the vertical axis (oldest at bottom, youngest at top — law of superposition) and rock properties on the horizontal axis
- **Width = grain size:** the column is widest for coarsest sediment (conglomerate), narrowest for finest (shale/mudstone); this creates the characteristic grain-size curve that allows fining- and coarsening-upward trends to be read at a glance
- **Standard lithological symbols (internationally agreed):**
  - Shale/mudstone: horizontal lines
  - Siltstone: dashes
  - Fine sandstone: fine dots
  - Coarse sandstone/grit: coarser dots
  - Conglomerate: circles/ovals (pebbles)
  - Limestone: brick pattern
  - Dolomite: rhombus pattern
  - Coal: solid black band
  - Volcanic ash/tuff: triangles or stippled band
- **Colour:** red/brown = oxidising terrestrial conditions; grey/black = reducing, oxygen-poor (marine or swamp); green = marine glauconite or chlorite; white/cream = carbonate or clean quartz sand

#### Contact types
- **Gradational contact:** the grain size or lithology changes progressively over centimetres to metres; indicates continuous deposition with a gradual environmental change (e.g., deepening or shallowing); drawn as a wavy line
- **Sharp contact:** an abrupt change in lithology or grain size with no transition; indicates a rapid environmental change or a brief hiatus; drawn as a straight line
- **Erosional contact:** a sharp contact with physical evidence of erosion — irregular or scoured base, rip-up clasts of the underlying lithology included in the base of the overlying unit; indicates a disconformity, a channel scour, or a turbidite base; drawn as a jagged line
- **Conformable vs. unconformable contacts:** a conformable contact (gradational or sharp) implies continuous deposition; an unconformable contact (erosional, often with angular discordance) implies a hiatus

#### Annotated features
- **Sedimentary structures** are drawn as symbols at the appropriate horizon: cross-bedding (chevron symbol), ripple lamination (wavy line), parallel lamination (dashes), hummocky cross-stratification (dome symbol), bioturbation (spiral symbol for Skolithos, etc.), mudcracks (inverted V), graded bedding (triangle tapering upward), load casts (blobs at base)
- **Fossils** are marked at their occurrence horizon: shell symbol for marine invertebrates, leaf symbol for plant material, fish symbol for vertebrates, circle for microfossils
- **Special features:** palaeosols (P symbol with rootlet traces), volcanic ash layers (V or tuff symbol), coal seams (solid black), nodules/concretions (oval symbols), colour changes
- **Scale bar** and **orientation** (facing direction, any structural dip corrections)
- Cards: Reading the Column (Layers, BRAND.gold), Contact Types (Mountain, BRAND.coral), Annotated Features (Clock, BRAND.amethyst)

### Section 3 — Application: "Reconstructing history from a composite section"

#### Reading fining- and coarsening-upward trends
- **Fining-upward:** grain size decreases upward; the column narrows toward the top; implies deepening water (transgression), abandonment of a high-energy channel, or waning flow; classic settings: fluvial point bar (coarse lag → cross-bedded sand → silty overbank mud); turbidite (graded Bouma sequence); transgressive marine sequence
- **Coarsening-upward:** grain size increases upward; the column widens toward the top; implies shallowing water (regression/progradation) or increasing energy; classic settings: delta front prograding over prodelta; shoreface building seaward; submarine fan lobe switching
- Repeated alternations of fining- then coarsening-upward packages = cyclic T-R sequences

#### Worked example: a composite column
Walk through a hypothetical 40 m composite section (bottom to top):
1. 0–5 m: grey-black shale, horizontal lamination, pyrite nodules, sparse marine microfossils → offshore anoxic shelf; deepwater, quiet, reducing
2. 5–12 m: interbedded siltstone and fine sandstone, hummocky cross-stratification, bioturbation by marine burrows → storm-influenced shoreface, between fair-weather and storm wave base
3. 12–18 m: medium sandstone, symmetric wave ripples, occasional shell fragments, sharp base → upper shoreface/beach, high energy wave zone
4. 18–19 m: sharp erosional contact with rip-up clasts of sandstone → sequence boundary or channel scour
5. 19–28 m: red-brown mudstone with carbonate nodules (caliche), root traces, desiccation cracks, fining upward from basal sand lag → terrestrial floodplain with palaeosol; major regression and exposure
6. 28–32 m: cross-bedded medium-coarse sandstone, scoured base, fining upward → fluvial channel fill
7. 32–40 m: gradational contact upward into grey marine shale with marine microfossils → renewed transgression over fluvial system

Interpretation: a complete regressive-transgressive cycle; bottom = offshore shelf; prograding shoreface = regression; erosional surface + floodplain palaeosol = subaerial exposure (sequence boundary); fluvial channel = lowstand; gradational upward to marine shale = next transgression (TST)

#### Correlation between sections
- Two or more columns from different localities can be correlated by matching distinctive horizons: a volcanic ash layer (same age everywhere), a unique fossil zone, a distinctive geochemical anomaly, or a recognisable sequence boundary
- Correlation demonstrates lateral continuity of facies or reveals lateral facies changes (the same time interval = different environments in different places)
- In industry, stratigraphic columns from wells are displayed side by side as "fence diagrams" or "correlation panels" to reconstruct the 3D geometry of reservoir and source intervals
- Card: Correlation and Interpretation (Layers, BRAND.jade)

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. On a standard stratigraphic column, what does column width represent, and why is this convention useful?
2. A stratigraphic column shows a sharp, irregular (jagged) contact with rip-up clasts of underlying mudstone at the base of an overlying sandstone. What type of contact is this and what does it indicate?
3. A column shows (bottom to top): fine sandstone with marine fossils → siltstone → grey shale → marine shale with diverse microfossils. The column narrows upward (grain size decreases). What does this succession record?
4. A column shows three repeated packages, each consisting of (base to top): grey offshore shale → hummocky sandstone → wave-rippled sandstone → sharp erosional surface. What process produces this pattern, and what is the significance of the repeated sharp erosional surfaces?
5. Two stratigraphic columns 50 km apart both contain a distinctive 10 cm thick white volcanic ash layer at different absolute heights within each section. How is this layer useful for correlation, and what can the difference in height between the two sections tell you?
