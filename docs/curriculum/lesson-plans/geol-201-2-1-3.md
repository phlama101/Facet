## Learning Objectives

1. Explain the physical basis of radiometric dating: radioactive decay as a constant-rate process and the decay equation relating parent/daughter ratio to time.
2. Describe the half-life concept and use it to estimate age from a given parent/daughter ratio.
3. Identify the major radiometric dating systems (K-Ar, Ar-Ar, Rb-Sr, Sm-Nd, U-Pb, ¹⁴C), their applicable materials, and their effective time ranges.
4. Read and interpret an isochron diagram (Rb-Sr) and a U-Pb concordia diagram at a conceptual level.
5. Identify the main sources of error in radiometric dating and explain how modern methods (isochrons, concordia) address them.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Nature's atomic clocks"
**Hook:** Every radioactive atom in every rock is ticking. Uranium-238 has been decaying to lead-206 since the solar system formed 4.567 billion years ago, at a rate so constant that it is indistinguishable from a perfect atomic clock. When Clair Patterson dated the Canyon Diablo meteorite in 1956 and obtained 4,550 ± 70 Ma, he was reading a clock that had been running for the entire history of the solar system, untouched, waiting for someone to read it. Radiometric dating did not just add numbers to the geologic timescale — it fundamentally changed what geology could ask and answer.

**Key terms:** radioactive decay, parent isotope, daughter isotope, half-life, decay constant (λ), isochron, concordia, closed system.

### Section 2 — Concept: "How radioactive decay becomes a clock"

#### The physics of decay
- Radioactive nuclei are unstable; they spontaneously transform into daughter nuclei by emitting particles (α, β) or by electron capture
- The decay rate is constant: each nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying per unit time, regardless of temperature, pressure, chemistry, or geological history — this is quantum mechanical and cannot be changed by any geological process
- Decay constant λ: the probability of decay per unit time; related to half-life by t½ = ln(2)/λ ≈ 0.693/λ
- The age equation: if D = number of daughter atoms, P = number of remaining parent atoms, D₀ = initial daughter atoms at time of closure, and t = time since closure:
  **D = D₀ + P(e^λt − 1)**
  Rearranging: **t = (1/λ) × ln(1 + (D − D₀)/P)**
- The initial daughter problem: D₀ must be known or estimated; this is the central challenge of all radiometric dating; different methods handle it differently (isochrons, known-zero-daughter systems)

#### Half-life and time ranges
- After one half-life: 50% of parent remains
- After two half-lives: 25% remains; after 10: ~0.1% remains
- Practical dating range: ~10 half-lives (too little parent remaining to measure accurately) to ~1/100 of a half-life (too little daughter to measure above analytical noise)
- Short-lived isotopes (¹⁴C, t½ = 5,730 yr) → useful for recent events up to ~50,000 yr
- Long-lived isotopes (²³⁸U, t½ = 4,468 Myr; ⁸⁷Rb, t½ = 48,800 Myr) → useful for geological and cosmological timescales

#### The major long-lived systems
- **K-Ar / Ar-Ar:** ⁴⁰K decays to ⁴⁰Ar (and ⁴⁰Ca); Ar is a gas that escapes molten rock but is trapped in crystals after solidification → zero-daughter starting condition for volcanic rocks; extremely widely used; Ar-Ar method irradiates samples to convert ³⁹K → ³⁹Ar, allowing measurement of ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar ratio and age from single mineral grains; records time of cooling through ~150–300°C (closure temperature varies by mineral)
- **Rb-Sr:** ⁸⁷Rb decays to ⁸⁷Sr; requires isochron approach (initial Sr is not zero); isochron plots ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr vs ⁸⁷Rb/⁸⁶Sr for co-genetic minerals or whole rocks; slope gives age, intercept gives initial ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr; powerful for crustal rocks and meteorites
- **Sm-Nd:** ¹⁴⁷Sm decays to ¹⁴³Nd; very long half-life (106,000 Myr); used for Archean and mantle rocks where Rb-Sr may have been reset; garnet and pyroxene strongly fractionate Sm/Nd → useful for dating metamorphic events
- **U-Pb:** ²³⁵U → ²⁰⁷Pb (t½ = 703.8 Myr) and ²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb (t½ = 4,468 Myr); two independent decay chains allow cross-checking via concordia diagram; zircon (ZrSiO₄) incorporates U but almost no Pb at crystallisation → excellent zero-daughter starting condition; most precise and widely trusted system; standard in modern geochronology
- **¹⁴C:** produced in upper atmosphere by cosmic ray bombardment of ¹⁴N; incorporated into living organisms through photosynthesis/food chains; after death, ¹⁴C decays (t½ = 5,730 yr) and is not replenished; date organic material up to ~50,000 yr; requires calibration against tree-ring chronologies (IntCal) for ¹⁴C/¹²C atmospheric variations

### Section 3 — Application: "Reading the clocks"

#### The isochron method (Rb-Sr example)
- Problem: we don't know the initial ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr when the rock formed
- Solution: plot multiple co-genetic minerals or whole rocks with different Rb/Sr ratios; at time of formation, all had the same ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr (because they crystallised from the same magma); through time, high-Rb/Sr minerals generate more ⁸⁷Sr than low-Rb/Sr minerals; on a plot of ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr (y-axis) vs ⁸⁷Rb/⁸⁶Sr (x-axis), the data define a line (isochron) whose slope = (e^λt − 1) → gives t; y-intercept = initial ⁸⁷Sr/⁸⁶Sr; if the data fall on a straight line, the closed-system assumption is validated
- Scatter from the isochron line indicates open-system behaviour (loss or gain of Rb or Sr after crystallisation) → the date is compromised

#### The concordia diagram (U-Pb)
- Two decay chains run simultaneously: ²³⁵U → ²⁰⁷Pb and ²³⁸U → ²⁰⁶Pb; they evolve at different rates
- Concordia curve: the locus of ²⁰⁷Pb/²³⁵U vs ²⁰⁶Pb/²³⁸U values expected for a closed system at any given age; a zircon that has remained closed since crystallisation plots ON the concordia curve; its position on the curve gives its age
- Discordance: if Pb was lost (e.g., by metamorphic heating), the zircon plots BELOW the concordia (discordant); multiple discordant zircons from the same sample define a chord (discordia line); the upper intercept with concordia gives the crystallisation age; the lower intercept gives the age of the Pb-loss event
- This dual-clock feature makes U-Pb the most robust dating system — it can detect and correct for open-system behaviour

#### Which method for which problem
- Volcanic rocks ≤ 50 ka → ¹⁴C if organic material is associated; K-Ar/Ar-Ar otherwise
- Volcanic and intrusive igneous rocks, any age → K-Ar/Ar-Ar (good) or U-Pb zircon (best)
- Metamorphic events → U-Pb (zircon rims or monazite); Sm-Nd (garnet-whole rock); Ar-Ar (white mica cooling ages)
- Sedimentary provenance → detrital zircon U-Pb (dates source, not deposition)
- Ancient crustal or mantle rocks → Sm-Nd; Lu-Hf
- Recent environmental and archaeological → ¹⁴C; U-Th for corals, speleothems

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. ³²Si has a half-life of ~150 years. A sample originally had 1,000 atoms of ³²Si. After 3 half-lives, how many remain, and what fraction of the original amount is that?
2. Why is the K-Ar system well-suited for dating volcanic rocks, and what is the key assumption required?
3. A Rb-Sr isochron plot shows three data points from co-genetic minerals scattered randomly and not defining a line. What does this indicate?
4. What is the concordia curve, and why does a zircon plotting below the concordia (discordant) not necessarily yield a useless result?
5. A researcher wants to date a 3-billion-year-old granite. They have a choice of ¹⁴C dating, K-Ar dating, and U-Pb zircon dating. Which should they choose and why?
