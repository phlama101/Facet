## Learning Objectives

2. Apply Walther's Law to predict vertical facies successions from lateral facies relationships.
3. Identify the characteristic sedimentary features of the main depositional environments: fluvial, deltaic, shallow marine, deep marine, and carbonate.
4. Explain how sea-level changes and subsidence control the stacking patterns of facies through time (transgressions and regressions).
5. Read a simplified stratigraphic column and infer the environmental history it records.

## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "Every rock layer is a snapshot"
**Hook:** Pick up any piece of limestone and you are holding a frozen snapshot of a shallow tropical sea — the chemistry of the water, the organisms that lived in it, even the direction of the waves that rippled across it. Pick up a red mudstone and you are holding a floodplain that dried and cracked under the sun perhaps 250 million years ago. Sedimentary rocks are not just records of time; they are records of place. Stratigraphy — the study of layered rocks — is the discipline that reads those records, reconstructing the geography and environment of ancient Earth from the rocks preserved beneath our feet.

**Key terms:** stratigraphy, sedimentary facies, depositional environment, Walther's Law, transgression, regression, accommodation space.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Facies and the environments that produce them"

#### The facies concept
- A **sedimentary facies** is a body of rock with a distinctive set of characteristics — grain size, mineralogy, sedimentary structures, and fossils — that reflects the environment in which it was deposited
- The concept was introduced by Amanz Gressly in 1838: rocks of the same age deposited in different environments have different facies
- Facies are the fundamental units of environmental interpretation; every sedimentary rock carries a facies signature
- Key principle: at any given moment, different facies are forming simultaneously in adjacent environments — a beach facies, a shallow shelf facies, a deep-water facies all accumulate at the same time, side by side

#### Walther's Law (1894)
- Johannes Walther observed: in a conformable vertical succession, the facies that occur above and below each other were once deposited laterally adjacent to each other
- Implication: if the shoreline migrates landward (transgression), shallow-marine facies will be deposited on top of beach/fluvial facies; if the shoreline migrates seaward (regression), the reverse occurs
- Walther's Law holds only where the vertical sequence is conformable (no missing time); unconformities break the law
- Practical power: knowing the lateral arrangement of modern environments (river → estuary → beach → shoreface → shelf → slope → deep sea) allows prediction of vertical facies successions in the rock record

#### Major depositional environments and diagnostic features
- **Fluvial (river):** fining-upward sequences (coarse lag → sand → overbank mud); cross-bedding from migrating dunes and bars; channel scour surfaces; red colouration from oxidising terrestrial conditions; plant fossils, root traces, caliche in overbank deposits
- **Deltaic:** coarsening-upward sequences (offshore mud → prodelta → delta front sand → distributary channel); combination of fluvial and marine indicators; wave ripples interbedded with river structures; abundant plant material; major coal-forming environments in the geological record
- **Shallow marine (beach, shoreface, shelf):** wave ripples (symmetric); hummocky cross-stratification from storm waves; bioturbation by marine invertebrates; marine shell fossils; glauconite in shelf muds; clean, well-sorted sand
- **Deep marine (slope, basin):** turbidites — graded beds deposited by turbidity currents (Bouma sequence: massive sand → parallel laminae → ripple laminae → upper parallel → hemipelagic mud); thin-bedded, fine-grained; rare bioturbation in distal turbidites; calcareous ooze in pelagic settings above the carbonate compensation depth (CCD)
- **Carbonate (reef, lagoon, tidal flat):** limestone and dolomite rather than siliciclastic sediment; skeletal grains (corals, crinoids, forams); ooids (concentrically layered carbonate grains forming in agitated, supersaturated water); stromatolites (microbial mats — dominant in Precambrian); mudcracked tidal flat limestones; reef core vs. fore-reef vs. back-reef facies association

#### Transgression, regression, and accommodation space
- **Accommodation space:** the space available for sediment to accumulate; controlled by subsidence (basin sinking) and eustasy (global sea level)
- **Transgression:** sea level rises relative to land → shoreline migrates landward → marine facies prograde over terrestrial/coastal facies; produces a coarsening-then-fining upward pattern; deepening-upward in marine sections
- **Regression:** sea level falls or sediment supply exceeds accommodation → shoreline migrates seaward → terrestrial and shallow facies prograde over deeper-water facies; shallowing-upward sequence
- Transgressive-regressive cycles (T-R cycles) are the fundamental building blocks of stratigraphy; they record the interplay of sea-level change, subsidence, and sediment supply through time

### Section 3 — Application: "Reconstructing ancient environments"

#### Walther's Law in practice
- Example: a vertical section shows (bottom to top) red mudstone with root traces → cross-bedded sandstone → fossiliferous limestone with wave ripples → black shale with marine microfossils
- Walther's Law interpretation: the section records a transgression — the shoreline migrated landward, replacing floodplain (red mudstone) → river channel (cross-bedded sand) → beach/shoreface (fossiliferous limestone) → open shelf (black shale)
- Each facies contact records a time when the depositional environment shifted

#### Reading a simplified stratigraphic column
- A stratigraphic column is a vertical diagram showing rock types (shown by standard lithological symbols), thickness, and interpreted environment from bottom to top
- Reading rules: (1) older rocks at the bottom, younger at top (law of superposition); (2) grain size shown by column width (wide = coarse, narrow = fine); (3) sedimentary structures and fossils annotated at their horizons; (4) interpreted environment labelled for each facies unit; (5) sharp contacts vs. gradational contacts indicate abrupt vs. gradual environmental change
- Worked example: a coarsening-upward package from mudstone → silty mudstone → hummocky cross-stratified sandstone → wave-rippled sandstone represents progradation of a shoreface (regression): the basin shallowed as sediment supply built out the shoreline
- Card: Stratigraphic Column Toolkit (Layers, BRAND.jade)

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. What is a sedimentary facies, and what does it tell you about the rock?
2. A vertical section shows (bottom to top): marine shale → limestone with marine fossils → cross-bedded sandstone → red mudstone with root traces. Apply Walther's Law: what does this sequence record?
3. A geologist describes a sandstone with symmetric wave ripples, hummocky cross-stratification, and abundant bioturbation by marine invertebrates. What depositional environment does this indicate?
4. What is accommodation space, and how does it change during a transgression?
5. A vertical succession shows three repeated packages, each consisting of (from base to top): black organic-rich shale → silty mudstone → hummocky sandstone → wave-rippled sandstone. What process is most likely responsible for the repetition of this pattern?
