## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Explain why minerals have consistent, identifiable physical properties** — because the crystalline lattice (established in 1.2.1) determines a mineral's bond geometry, which in turn determines every measurable physical property.
2. **Use the Mohs hardness scale** — recall the 10 reference minerals in order, apply everyday reference points (fingernail ~2.5, copper coin ~3.5, glass ~5.5, steel file ~6.5), and use scratch tests to bracket an unknown mineral's hardness.
3. **Explain why streak is more reliable than surface colour for identification** — mineral colour varies with trace impurities; streak (the colour of powdered mineral on an unglazed porcelain plate) reflects the pure mineral's true pigment.
4. **Distinguish cleavage from fracture** — cleavage is breakage along flat planes defined by weak bonds in the crystal lattice; fracture is irregular breakage in any other direction.
5. **Recognise the main luster types** (metallic, vitreous, waxy, pearly, silky) and identify which luster type applies to a given mineral description.

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "Reading the clues"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Imagine you are handed a fragment of an unknown mineral — no label, no context. Using just a fingernail, a copper coin, a piece of glass, a steel nail file, and a small piece of unglazed porcelain tile, an experienced geologist can narrow it down to a handful of candidates within minutes. This is mineral identification in the field, and it is built on a single insight from Lesson 1.2.1: because every sample of a given mineral has the same crystalline lattice, every sample has the same physical properties. That consistency is what makes identification possible.

**What this section establishes.**
- The logical bridge from 1.2.1: crystalline structure → consistent properties → identifiable properties.
- The idea that identification is a toolkit of tests, not a single test.
- The four key vocabulary terms for the lesson.
- Why experienced geologists can identify most minerals with no laboratory equipment.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Hardness** — a mineral's resistance to being scratched, determined by the strength of bonds in the crystal lattice. Measured on the Mohs scale from 1 (softest) to 10 (hardest).
- **Streak** — the colour of a mineral when powdered; determined by rubbing the mineral across an unglazed porcelain plate. More reliable than surface colour because trace impurities affect surface colour but not the powdered mineral's fundamental pigment.
- **Cleavage** — the tendency of a mineral to break along flat, smooth planes in specific directions, controlled by planes of weaker chemical bonds in the crystal lattice. Cleavage planes are reproducible: every break in that direction produces the same smooth surface.
- **Luster** — the quality and character of light reflected from a mineral's surface. The two broadest categories are metallic (mirror-like, opaque, like metals) and non-metallic (which includes vitreous/glassy, waxy, pearly, silky, and others).

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The identification toolkit
**Type:** `concept` (with three cards)
**Estimated reading time:** ~8–9 minutes

**Subsection A — Why consistency makes identification possible.** In Lesson 1.2.1, we established that every sample of a given mineral has the same crystalline structure. That shared structure means the same bond types and bond strengths throughout, which in turn means the same resistance to scratching, the same tendency to break in particular directions, the same way light interacts with the surface. A sample of quartz from Brazil and a sample from Norway are chemically and structurally identical, so they behave identically in every test. This is the fundamental reason mineral identification works: you are not guessing at an individual sample's peculiarities — you are measuring a universal property of that mineral species.

**Subsection B — Hardness and the Mohs scale.** Hardness is defined as resistance to scratching, not to breaking — a common confusion. A hard mineral can be brittle (it shatters) but difficult to scratch. The Mohs scale, developed by Friedrich Mohs in 1812, arranges ten reference minerals from softest (1) to hardest (10): talc (1), gypsum (2), calcite (3), fluorite (4), apatite (5), orthoclase feldspar (6), quartz (7), topaz (8), corundum (9), diamond (10). The scale is relative and ordinal, not linear: the actual difference in absolute hardness between diamond (10) and corundum (9) is far greater than the difference between calcite (3) and gypsum (2). A harder mineral will always scratch a softer one — this is the test.

Everyday reference points make field testing easy without carrying all ten reference minerals: a **fingernail** is approximately 2.5; a **copper coin** is approximately 3.5; a **glass plate** is approximately 5.5; a **steel nail file** is approximately 6.5. To determine an unknown mineral's hardness, try scratching it with each of these in sequence. If a mineral scratches glass (5.5) but is scratched by a steel file (6.5), its hardness is between 5.5 and 6.5 — which matches orthoclase feldspar (6). Always test in both directions: scratch the unknown mineral with the reference object, and also scratch the reference object with the unknown mineral, to confirm which is harder.

**Subsection C — Streak.** Streak is the colour of a mineral's powder. When you drag a mineral across an unglazed porcelain plate (which has a hardness of about 6.5), the mineral is abraded and leaves a powdered line — the streak. The colour of that powder is often dramatically different from the colour of the intact mineral surface, and it is more consistent.

Why? The colour of a mineral's outer surface can be affected by trace chemical impurities, oxidation, or tarnish. Quartz, for example, comes in many colours — clear, purple (amethyst), pink (rose quartz), yellow (citrine), smoky brown — depending on trace amounts of different impurities. But all of these varieties produce a white streak, because the streak reflects the mineral's fundamental structural pigment rather than surface effects. Pyrite (fool's gold) is a striking example: its surface looks metallic gold, leading to centuries of mistaken identification — but its streak is a distinctive greenish-black. Hematite looks black or metallic grey at the surface but has a characteristic brick-red streak. In field geology, streak is often checked immediately after first observing colour, precisely because colour misleads and streak does not.

**Subsection D — Cleavage and fracture.** When a mineral breaks, it breaks in one of two ways. **Cleavage** is breakage along flat, parallel planes that correspond to directions of weaker chemical bonds in the crystal lattice. Because the lattice is repeating and ordered, these planes of weakness run straight through the crystal in consistent directions. Every time you break the mineral in that direction, you get the same flat, smooth surface. Calcite has three directions of cleavage at specific angles, which is why a calcite crystal — regardless of its original shape — can always be cleaved into a characteristic rhombohedron. Mica has one perfect cleavage plane, which is why it splits into thin, flat sheets. Feldspar has two cleavage directions at nearly right angles, producing blocky fragments.

**Fracture** is what happens when a mineral breaks in a direction that is not a cleavage plane — when the bonds are more uniformly strong or when the mineral has no organised lattice (as in mineraloids). Quartz has no cleavage: its bonds are equally strong in all directions, so it breaks irregularly, producing curved, shell-like surfaces called **conchoidal fracture** (the same pattern as broken glass). Obsidian also shows conchoidal fracture — and this property was exploited by prehistoric toolmakers, who could shape obsidian into sharp-edged blades by controlled fracturing.

Cleavage and fracture are closely related to hardness: both are consequences of bond strength within the crystal lattice. A mineral can exhibit both: it may cleave in some directions and fracture in others.

**Subsection E — Luster.** Luster describes the quality of light reflected from a mineral's surface — not the colour, but the way the surface handles light. The two primary categories are **metallic** (shiny, opaque, like polished metal — galena, pyrite, native copper) and **non-metallic** (translucent or transparent, reflecting light in other ways). Non-metallic lusters include:
- **Vitreous** (glassy) — the most common; quartz, glass, most silicate minerals
- **Waxy** — soft shine like candle wax; flint, serpentine
- **Pearly** — iridescent, like the inside of a shell; mica, talc, some calcite
- **Silky** — fine parallel fibres reflecting light; fibrous gypsum (satin spar)
- **Resinous** — like solidified resin; amber (which is not a mineral, but the luster type exists in some)
- **Adamantine** — brilliant, extremely high reflectivity; diamond

Luster alone rarely identifies a mineral uniquely, but combined with hardness, streak, and cleavage, it narrows the field quickly.

**Subsection F — Colour, crystal form, and specific gravity (brief).** Several other properties supplement the main four. **Colour** is the most obvious feature but the least reliable identifier — use it as a starting point, not a conclusion (remember that quartz comes in many colours). **Crystal form** refers to the external shape produced by the internal lattice: cubes (halite, galena), hexagonal prisms (quartz), rhombohedra (calcite). When well-formed crystals are present, form can be immediately diagnostic. **Specific gravity** — how much heavier a mineral is compared to an equal volume of water — is useful for distinguishing dense minerals (galena: SG ~7.6; gold: SG ~19.3) from light ones (quartz: SG ~2.65) when other tests are ambiguous.

**Subsection G — Using the toolkit together.** No single property uniquely identifies every mineral; the power comes from combining them. A systematic approach: observe luster first (metallic vs. non-metallic); test hardness with everyday tools; check streak on a porcelain plate; examine how the mineral breaks (cleavage planes, fracture pattern); note colour and any special properties (effervescence in acid for calcite; magnetism in magnetite). With four or five properties recorded, cross-referencing a field guide or reference table will narrow most common minerals to one or two candidates.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Mohs hardness application** — a mineral scratches glass (~5.5) but cannot scratch a steel file (~6.5); what is its approximate Mohs hardness? (between 5.5 and 6.5 → ~6, consistent with orthoclase feldspar; distractors misapply the scratch-test logic)
2. **Streak reliability** — why is streak a more reliable identification property than surface colour? (surface colour varies with trace impurities; streak reflects the mineral's fundamental pigment; distractors confuse the properties or give incorrect reasons)
3. **Cleavage vs. fracture** — a mineral breaks into flat, smooth-surfaced fragments that always meet at the same angle, no matter how many times it is broken; which property does this describe? (cleavage — breakage along lattice-defined planes of weakness; distractors describe fracture or confuse with hardness)

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"Why can a geologist identify a mineral with a fingernail and a coin? How is that rigorous science?"**
   *Resolved by:* The crystalline-structure foundation (1.2.1 callback) and the Mohs hardness subsection. Everyday objects serve as calibrated reference points because the Mohs scale is reproducible — a fingernail will always scratch gypsum and always fail to scratch calcite, regardless of where the minerals formed.

2. **"Quartz can be purple, pink, yellow, or clear — so how is colour useful for identification at all?"**
   *Resolved by:* The streak subsection. Surface colour is unreliable; streak is reliable. All varieties of quartz produce a white streak. This is why streak is checked in addition to colour, not instead of it.

3. **"A mineral breaks into flat shards with a consistent angle between faces. What is happening inside the crystal?"**
   *Resolved by:* The cleavage subsection. The flat planes correspond to directions of weaker bonds in the lattice. The lattice is the same throughout, so those weak-bond planes run parallel across the whole crystal, producing the same flat surface every time.
