## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The lottery of preservation"
**Hook:** For every organism preserved as a fossil, billions were not. The fossil record is not a random sample of ancient life — it is the result of a series of filters, each one eliminating most organisms from the record before the next filter has a chance to act. An animal dies. Its soft parts decay within days. Scavengers disperse the skeleton. Currents transport the bones. Waves abrade them. Burial is required before permineralisation can begin, but burial itself requires the right depositional environment. Even after burial, diagenesis may dissolve or recrystallise the fossil. Uplift and erosion may eventually destroy it. The fossils that survive to be collected by a palaeontologist are an extremely small, extremely biased sample of the organisms that once lived. Understanding the filters — the science of **taphonomy** — is essential to interpreting what the fossil record actually tells us about ancient life.

**Key terms:** taphonomy, permineralisation, replacement, mould, cast, carbonisation, preservation potential, taphonomic bias, Lagerstätten.

### Section 2 — Concept: "From death to fossil"

#### Taphonomy: the study of preservation
- **Taphonomy** (from Greek: taphos = burial, nomos = law) is the study of all processes that affect organic remains from the moment of death to the moment of discovery — decomposition, transport, burial, diagenesis, and exhumation
- Introduced by Ivan Yefremov (1940); now a major subdiscipline of palaeontology
- Three main stages: (1) **Biostratinomy** — processes between death and burial: decay, scavenging, transport, abrasion, disarticulation; (2) **Diagenesis** — processes during and after burial: mineral replacement, compaction, recrystallisation, dissolution; (3) **Exhumation** — erosion and exposure at the surface; the "last filter" before collection
- Most organisms are destroyed in stage 1; only a tiny fraction ever reach burial; of those buried, diagenesis destroys many more

#### Pathways of fossilisation
- **Permineralisation:** mineral-rich groundwater infiltrates pore spaces in bones, shells, or wood and precipitates minerals (silica, calcite, pyrite, iron oxides) within the voids; the original material may remain or may be partially replaced; most common mode of bone and wood preservation; produces "petrified wood" and most vertebrate fossils
- **Replacement:** the original hard-part material is dissolved and replaced, molecule by molecule, by a different mineral — silica replacing calcite in some marine invertebrates, pyrite replacing original shell in anaerobic settings; the external shape is preserved but the original chemistry is gone; pyritised ammonites and silicified brachiopods are examples
- **Mould and cast:** the organism is buried, then the organic or carbonate material dissolves, leaving a cavity (the **external mould**) that records the shape of the organism's outer surface; if sediment or minerals later fill the mould, the result is a **cast** that reproduces the exterior shape; internal moulds (steinkerns) form when sediment fills the interior of a shell before the shell dissolves
- **Carbonisation (coalification):** soft tissues — leaves, fish scales, integument — are slowly compressed and their volatile compounds driven off, leaving a thin carbon film on the bedding plane; most common in fine-grained lacustrine or marine shale; produces beautiful preservation of soft anatomy including outlines of skin, feathers, and internal organs in some Lagerstätten
- **Amber preservation:** resin from coniferous trees traps organisms (insects, spiders, small vertebrates, plant material) and polymerises into amber; protects against decay and compression; preserves original tissue chemistry, DNA (rarely, up to tens of millions of years old), and three-dimensional external morphology; also captures ancient atmosphere in trapped air bubbles
- **Exceptional physical preservation:** permafrost freezing (woolly mammoths, Pleistocene megafauna — soft tissue, hair, stomach contents preserved); desiccation in caves or arid environments; entombment in volcanic ash (Pompeii-style casts)

### Section 3 — Application: "Biases in the fossil record"

#### Preservation potential
- Hard parts (bones, shells, teeth, wood) have much higher preservation potential than soft tissues; the vast majority of animal phyla have some members with hard parts, but many entirely soft-bodied groups (annelid worms, jellyfishes, most plankton) are almost invisible in the record
- Marine invertebrates with carbonate shells are the most abundantly preserved organisms; their record is far more complete than that of land animals
- Shallow marine environments have the highest preservation potential: low energy, steady sedimentation, absence of the oxidising conditions that destroy organics; deep-sea environments, despite covering 70% of the ocean floor, have poor preservation because carbonate dissolves below the CCD
- Terrestrial environments have low preservation potential: oxidising conditions, biological activity, erosion; river-transported material is fragmented and abrades; forests rarely preserve as fossils; the terrestrial fossil record is much patchier than the marine record

#### Biases in the fossil record
- **Taxonomic bias:** organisms with hard parts are over-represented; the "Cambrian Explosion" partially reflects the appearance of hard-part skeletonisation, not just a genuine explosion of diversity
- **Environmental bias:** shallow marine environments are over-represented; open-ocean and deep-sea biotas are almost unknown before the Mesozoic; terrestrial biotas are spottily recorded
- **Temporal bias:** recent strata are more complete than ancient strata — more rock survives, more outcrop area exists, more fossils have been collected; the fossil record becomes increasingly incomplete back in time
- **Geographic bias:** most fossil collections come from accessible outcrops in North America, Europe, and China; tropical and polar biotas are under-sampled
- **Collector bias:** large, interesting organisms are preferentially collected; microscopic organisms are under-sampled unless specifically targeted
- Implication: raw fossil diversity counts underestimate true ancient diversity, especially for soft-bodied organisms, open-ocean species, and deep-time taxa; corrections for these biases are a major active research area

#### Introduction to Lagerstätten
- German: Lagerstätte = "mother lode" or "storage place"; plural Lagerstätten
- Exceptional preservation sites where soft tissues or delicate structures are preserved — feathers, eyes, guts, muscles, skin — in addition to hard parts; provide windows into ancient ecosystems invisible in the normal fossil record
- Two types: **Konzentrat-Lagerstätten** (concentration deposits — abundant hard-part material, exceptional quantity) and **Konservat-Lagerstätten** (conservation deposits — exceptional quality of soft-tissue preservation)
- Examples of Konservat-Lagerstätten: Burgess Shale (Canada, ~508 Ma — Cambrian soft-bodied fauna); Maotianshan Shales/Chengjiang (China, ~520 Ma — earliest animal fossils); Solnhofen Limestone (Germany, ~150 Ma — Archaeopteryx and flying reptiles); Messel Pit (Germany, ~47 Ma — Eocene mammals); Yixian Formation (China, ~125 Ma — feathered dinosaurs)
- Detailed coverage in lesson 2.3.4

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. What is taphonomy, and why is it important for interpreting the fossil record?
2. A palaeontologist finds a perfectly shaped bivalve shell in Devonian limestone, but chemical analysis shows the shell is now made of silica rather than the original calcite. What type of preservation is this?
3. Why do marine invertebrates with carbonate shells have much higher preservation potential than soft-bodied terrestrial organisms?
4. The Cambrian fossil record shows an apparent sudden explosion of diverse animal life. How might taphonomic bias complicate the interpretation of this pattern?
5. An insect is found preserved in amber with intact wing venation, leg setae, and even traces of internal organs. What conditions made this extraordinary preservation possible?
