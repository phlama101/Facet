## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The map that moves"

**Hook:** In 1912, Alfred Wegener published a radical idea: the continents were not fixed in place. South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces. Identical fossils of the freshwater reptile Mesosaurus appeared on both sides of the Atlantic — an animal that could not have swum across an ocean. Ancient glacial deposits in tropical Africa matched those in South America and India. Wegener called his hypothesis continental drift. The geological establishment ridiculed him for decades — not because the evidence was wrong, but because he had no mechanism to explain how continents could plough through the ocean floor. It would take fifty years, a world war, and the invention of ocean-floor mapping to prove him right.

**Key Terms:**
- **Plate tectonics** — The theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into a mosaic of rigid plates that move relative to one another, driven by heat from Earth's interior. Plate interactions at boundaries produce earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain ranges, and ocean trenches.
- **Lithosphere** — The rigid outer layer of Earth, comprising the crust and the uppermost mantle. The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates. Average thickness: ~100 km under oceans, up to ~200 km under old continental cratons.
- **Asthenosphere** — The partially molten, mechanically weak layer of the upper mantle directly below the lithosphere, extending to ~660 km depth. It flows slowly under sustained stress, allowing the rigid lithospheric plates above to move across it.
- **Continental drift** — Wegener's 1912 hypothesis that continents had once been joined in a supercontinent (Pangaea) and had since drifted apart. Lacked a mechanism; vindicated by seafloor spreading in the 1960s.
- **Seafloor spreading** — The process by which new oceanic crust is created at mid-ocean ridges as magma rises from the mantle, solidifies, and is pushed laterally away from the ridge. The discovery of seafloor spreading provided the mechanism continental drift lacked.

### Section 2 — Concept: "Evidence and mechanism"

**Body overview:**
- Wegener's four evidence lines: continental fit, matching geology across ocean basins, identical fossil assemblages, paleoclimate anomalies (coal in Antarctica, glacial deposits in Africa)
- Why rejected: no known force strong enough to move continents through solid ocean floor
- Harry Hess (1960): seafloor spreading — mid-ocean ridges as sites of new crust creation
- Paleomagnetic evidence: magnetic reversal stripes symmetric about mid-ocean ridges confirm spreading
- Age of ocean floor: youngest at ridges, oldest near continents — maximum ~200 Ma (vs continental rocks up to 4 Ga)
- The plate tectonic synthesis (1960s–1970s): lithosphere divided into ~15 major plates
- Lithosphere vs asthenosphere: rigid plates "float" on weak, slowly flowing asthenosphere
- Mantle convection: heat-driven circulation in the mantle drives plate motion (ridge push + slab pull)
- Rock cycle connection: plate tectonics controls where igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks form

### Section 3 — Quiz: 3 Questions

**Q1:** Wegener's continental drift hypothesis was supported by multiple lines of evidence but rejected by most geologists for decades. Which of the following best explains why — and what eventually resolved the debate?
- A) Wegener's evidence was fabricated — the fossil and geological matches were later shown to be coincidental, and the theory was only accepted after entirely new evidence was gathered
- B) Wegener had compelling geological and fossil evidence but no mechanism to explain how continents moved; seafloor spreading, discovered in the 1960s, provided the missing mechanism and vindicated the hypothesis ✓
- C) The theory was rejected on political grounds during the Cold War; acceptance came when international scientific cooperation resumed
- D) Wegener's evidence was valid but only applied to the Southern Hemisphere; new evidence from the Northern Hemisphere was needed to confirm a global theory

**Q2:** Paleomagnetic surveys of the ocean floor reveal symmetric stripes of alternating normal and reversed magnetic polarity on both sides of every mid-ocean ridge, with the youngest rocks at the ridge crest and progressively older rocks away from it. What do these patterns demonstrate?
- A) The ocean floor is stationary; the magnetic stripes record lateral variations in mantle composition that produce different magnetic signatures across the seafloor
- B) The ocean floor is older near the continents and younger near the ridges because sediment accumulation near continents resets the magnetic signature of the rock
- C) New oceanic crust forms continuously at mid-ocean ridges as magma solidifies and records Earth's magnetic field at the time of cooling; the crust then spreads symmetrically away from the ridge, producing mirror-image magnetic stripes that confirm seafloor spreading ✓
- D) Mid-ocean ridges are sites of subduction, not spreading — the stripes record the destruction of crust as it descends into the mantle, producing alternating zones of heated and cooled rock

**Q3:** Oceanic crust subducts beneath continental crust at convergent plate boundaries. What property of oceanic crust makes it subduct rather than override the continent?
- A) Oceanic crust is thinner than continental crust, so it is weaker and bends downward under the weight of the continental plate pushing against it
- B) Oceanic crust is composed of mafic rock (basalt and gabbro) and is denser (~3.0 g/cm³) than continental crust (~2.7 g/cm³), which is felsic (granitic); the denser oceanic plate sinks into the mantle when it meets the less dense continental plate ✓
- C) Oceanic crust subducts because it is always older than continental crust and has had more time to cool and contract, making it heavier
- D) Oceanic crust is pulled down by the weight of ocean water above it — the hydrostatic pressure of the ocean forces the crust to subduct at continental margins
