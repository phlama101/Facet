## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **State and apply the five criteria** that define a mineral — naturally occurring, inorganic, solid, definite chemical composition, crystalline structure — and use all five to evaluate whether a given substance qualifies.
2. **Distinguish minerals from rocks** (a rock is an aggregate of one or more minerals), **mineraloids** (amorphous solids lacking a crystalline structure, such as opal and obsidian), and **organic materials** (coal, amber, pearls) that fail the inorganic criterion.
3. **Explain why crystalline structure matters** — that the regular, repeating atomic arrangement gives every sample of a given mineral the same consistent physical properties, regardless of where it formed.
4. **Name at least four common minerals with their chemical formulas**: quartz (SiO₂), calcite (CaCO₃), halite (NaCl), and olivine (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄.

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "Not just any solid"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Pick up a piece of granite. It looks uniform — a rock. But look closely, and you will see distinct specks of different colours: pale pink, translucent white, glittery black. Each speck is a different substance with its own chemistry, its own crystal structure, and its own set of physical properties. Those specks are minerals. The granite itself is not a mineral — it is a mixture. Understanding the difference between a mineral and a rock is the first step to understanding how Earth's materials work.

**What this section establishes.**
- The everyday confusion between "mineral" and "rock" — and why the distinction matters.
- Connection to Module 1.1: the crust and mantle we explored in the last four lessons are ultimately assemblages of mineral grains. Naming those layers as "silicate rock" was shorthand for "rock made of silicate minerals."
- Preview of the five criteria that will be defined in the concept section.
- The key vocabulary: mineral, crystal, chemical formula, crystalline structure.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Mineral** — a naturally occurring, inorganic, solid substance with a definite chemical composition and an ordered crystalline (atomic) structure. A substance must meet all five criteria to qualify.
- **Crystal** — a solid in which atoms are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional pattern. This internal order is what gives minerals their characteristic and consistent physical properties.
- **Chemical formula** — a notation that specifies the types and ratios of atoms in a substance. Quartz, for example, always has the formula SiO₂ — one silicon atom for every two oxygen atoms — no matter where it formed.
- **Mineraloid** — a naturally occurring, inorganic solid that resembles a mineral but lacks a fully ordered crystalline structure. Opal and obsidian are mineraloids; they fail the "crystalline" criterion.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The five criteria and the edge cases
**Type:** `concept` (with five cards, one per criterion)
**Estimated reading time:** ~8–9 minutes

**Subsection A — Why a precise definition?** Geology is full of solid materials: table salt, coal, amber, volcanic glass, synthetic ruby, ice, pearl. All of them are solid. Some of them are crystalline. Some occur in nature. Which ones are minerals? Without a precise definition, the answer is arbitrary. The five-criteria definition exists because mineralogists need a consistent, universal standard — one that works whether you are classifying a substance found in the Arctic, in a deep ocean vent, or on Mars.

**Subsection B — The five criteria, one at a time.**
- **Naturally occurring**: must form through geological processes, not in a laboratory or factory. Synthetic diamonds — chemically identical to natural diamonds — are not minerals. Neither is laboratory-grown silicon, even though natural silicon exists as a mineral.
- **Inorganic**: must not be produced primarily by living organisms. Coal is organic — it formed from compressed plant material. Amber is fossilised tree resin. Pearls are produced by molluscs. None of these are minerals, even though they are solid and natural.
- **Solid**: liquids and gases are excluded. Liquid mercury occurs naturally and is inorganic, but it is not a mineral because it is not solid. Notably, **ice is a mineral** — it is naturally occurring, inorganic, solid, has the formula H₂O, and has a crystalline structure (the hexagonal symmetry responsible for snowflake shapes).
- **Definite chemical composition**: must have a fixed or narrowly restricted chemical formula. Quartz is always SiO₂. Halite (table salt) is always NaCl. Calcite is always CaCO₃. Some minerals allow limited substitution of one element for a similar one (such as olivine, where iron and magnesium swap positions), but the overall formula structure remains consistent. Coal, by contrast, has a highly variable composition depending on its source — another reason it fails.
- **Crystalline structure**: the atoms must be arranged in an ordered, repeating three-dimensional lattice. This internal geometry determines a mineral's hardness, cleavage, optical properties, and shape. Obsidian is a naturally occurring, inorganic, solid volcanic glass with a consistent composition — but its atoms are arranged randomly, like a frozen liquid. It is a **mineraloid**, not a mineral.

**Subsection C — Why the crystalline criterion matters.** A mineral's internal atomic arrangement is not just a technicality. It is the reason every sample of quartz on Earth has the same hardness (7 on the Mohs scale), the same tendency to break in predictable patterns (conchoidal fracture), the same melting point, and the same optical behaviour. It is what makes identification possible: a geologist can pick up a clear, glassy mineral anywhere in the world and — by testing a few physical properties — determine whether it is quartz or calcite or topaz, because the crystal structure guarantees consistency. A material without crystalline structure has no such guarantee.

**Subsection D — Minerals and rocks distinguished.** A **rock** is an aggregate — a mixture — of one or more minerals bonded together. Granite is a rock; it contains quartz, feldspar, and mica. Limestone is a rock; it contains primarily calcite. A single, pure mineral grain is not a rock. The boundary matters in geology because minerals and rocks are studied differently: mineralogy examines the properties and classification of individual mineral species; petrology examines rocks as whole assemblages and the processes that form them.

**Subsection E — Common minerals and their formulas.** Only about 30 mineral species make up the overwhelming majority of Earth's crust and mantle. A few examples to anchor the concept:
- **Quartz** (SiO₂) — glassy, hard, abundant in continental crust and sand
- **Calcite** (CaCO₃) — the main mineral in limestone and marble
- **Halite** (NaCl) — table salt, forms by evaporation of seawater
- **Olivine** ((Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄) — the dominant mineral in the upper mantle; the source of most mantle heat-transfer rocks
- **Feldspar** (complex silicate, varies) — the most abundant mineral group in Earth's crust

These will all be explored in detail in Lesson 1.2.3 (The Rock-Forming Minerals). For now, knowing that they exist and have fixed formulas is enough.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Application of all five criteria** — given four substances, identify which one is a mineral (correct answer passes all five tests; distractors each fail at least one criterion in a different way)
2. **Ice as a mineral / water as not** — why does ice qualify when liquid water does not? (solid + crystalline criteria; tests understanding of those two criteria specifically)
3. **Mineraloid vs. mineral** — why is obsidian NOT a mineral despite being naturally occurring, inorganic, and solid? (lacks crystalline structure — it is amorphous volcanic glass)

Each answer choice — correct and all distractors — receives an explanation within the single `explain` field.

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"What exactly makes something a mineral — isn't it just any rock-like solid?"**
   *Resolved by:* The five-criteria breakdown. Five specific, testable criteria must all be met simultaneously; most common "rock-like" substances fail at least one.

2. **"Why does it matter that minerals have a crystalline structure? Isn't that just a technicality?"**
   *Resolved by:* Subsection C — the crystalline lattice is the reason all samples of a mineral have the same physical properties worldwide, making identification possible and consistent.

3. **"Is ice a mineral? Is glass? Is coal?"**
   *Resolved by:* The edge-case discussion in Subsection B and the quiz — ice qualifies (all five criteria met); obsidian/glass does not (amorphous, not crystalline); coal does not (organic origin).

## 6. Connection to surrounding lessons

### Previous lesson — 1.1.4 / Module 1.1 close
Module 1.1 described Earth's interior as built from "silicate rock" and "iron-nickel alloy" without defining those materials at the mineral level. This lesson bridges from that planetary-scale view to the material scale: the "silicate rock" of the crust is made of silicate minerals (quartz, feldspar, olivine); the "silicate rock" of the mantle is mainly olivine and pyroxene. Minerals are the atoms-and-molecules level of the geological hierarchy.
