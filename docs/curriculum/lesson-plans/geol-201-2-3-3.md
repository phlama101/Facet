## Section Outline

### Section 1 — Intro: "The behavior preserved in stone"
**Hook:** A single theropod footprint in a Cretaceous lake bed tells you more about how a dinosaur moved than a complete skeleton can. The footprint records gait, stride length, speed (estimated from stride/hip-height ratios), and body weight. It records where the animal was standing and in what direction it was walking. It records the consistency of the substrate — was the sediment firm, soft, waterlogged? The skeleton tells you what the animal looked like. The footprint tells you what it was doing.

Trace fossils — the preserved record of biological activity rather than biological remains — are the most under-appreciated evidence in the entire fossil record. They include footprints and trackways, burrows and borings, crawling trails, feeding scribbles on seafloor mud, and fossilised feces. Unlike body fossils, they cannot be transported: they record in situ behavior, in the actual environment where the organism lived. They record the soft-bodied organisms that left no body fossils at all. And they extend the record of animal behavior into the Precambrian — into the time before animals had hard parts to preserve.

**Key terms:** ichnology, ichnofossil (trace fossil), ichnospecies, ichnofacies, bioturbation, biogenic sedimentary structures, substrate.

### Section 2 — Concept: "The vocabulary of trace fossils"

#### What ichnology studies
- **Ichnology**: the study of traces produced by organisms — living (neoichnology) or fossil (paleoichnology / ichnopaleontology)
- Trace fossils record **behavior**, not organisms: a trace fossil is a sedimentary structure created by an organism's activity rather than a remnant of the organism itself
- Critical implication: different organisms can produce identical traces (convergent behavior); one organism can produce multiple different traces in different activities — traces are named as **ichnospecies** (Skolithos linearis, Cruziana rugosa) independently of the producer, following the same binomial nomenclature as body fossils but classified in an entirely separate Linnaean framework
- Trace fossils are usually **in situ** — not transported — making them reliable records of the actual depositional environment; body fossils may be transported far from where the organism lived

#### Categories of trace fossils
- **Tracks and trackways**: individual footprints (tracks) or a series of successive footprints (trackways); preserve gait, stride, and locomotion; produced by walking, running, hopping, or swimming animals; from Cambrian arthropod tracks to Carboniferous tetrapod tracks to Cretaceous dinosaur trackways; speed can be estimated using the Alexander equation: speed ∝ √(g × stride length) / hip height
- **Trails**: continuous surface markings left by an organism dragging its body or appendages across a soft sediment surface; produced by annelid worms, molluscs, arthropods; examples: Helminthoidea (winding worm trail), Nereites (complex winding trail with lateral feeding traces)
- **Burrows**: excavations in soft sediment (unconsolidated sand, mud); the most abundant category; classified by orientation and function: **vertical burrows** are typically dwelling or escape structures (Skolithos — cylindrical vertical tube); **horizontal burrows** are typically feeding or locomotion structures (Planolites — simple horizontal tube; Chondrites — branching dendritic burrow penetrating into sediment to access organic material); **complex 3D structures** combine dwelling and systematic feeding (Thalassinoides — boxwork burrow system of ghost shrimps and crabs in shallow marine settings; Ophiomorpha — pellet-lined burrow of ghost shrimps)
- **Borings**: excavations in hard substrates — rock, shell, bone, or wood; require harder tools (rasping organs, chemical dissolution) than soft-sediment burrowers; examples: Trypanites (cylindrical boring in hard rock, produced by polychaete worms and sponges); Gastrochaenolites (flask-shaped boring in shell or rock, produced by boring bivalves); Entobia (sponge boring forming irregular network of chambers in carbonate rock); borings record **bioerosion** of hard substrates and provide evidence of rocky shorelines, shell banks, and submarine hard grounds
- **Feeding traces and grazing trails**: systematic patterns left by organisms harvesting organic material from the sediment surface or shallow subsurface; most elaborate in the deep sea where organic material is scarce; **graphoglyptids** — complex spiral, meandering, or branching patterns on deep-sea turbidite surfaces (Spirorhaphe, Cosmorhaphe, Paleodictyon) — record systematic mining strategies that maximise the area of sediment harvested with minimal overlap; present and complex from the Cambrian onward
- **Coprolites**: fossilised feces; preserve diet (bone fragments, scales, plant material identifiable inside), gut parasites (eggs of helminths), and predator-prey relationships; range from Cambrian to Pleistocene; often the only evidence of feeding behavior for organisms that left no other trace

### Section 3 — Application: "Ichnofacies: reading environments from traces"

#### The Seilacher ichnofacies model
- **Ichnofacies**: a recurrent, characteristic association of trace fossils that indicates a particular depositional environment — defined by the combination of trace types, bioturbation intensity, diversity, and depth distribution
- Developed by Adolf Seilacher (1967); expanded and refined ever since; remains the primary tool for using trace fossils to interpret ancient depositional environments
- The model works because trace-maker communities (deposit feeders, suspension feeders, predators) respond to environmental gradients (energy, oxygen, substrate firmness, food availability) in predictable ways; similar environments produce similar trace assemblages regardless of the geologic age

#### Five classic marine ichnofacies
1. **Skolithos ichnofacies**: high-energy, unstable substrate, shallow marine (beach to shoreface above storm wave base); vertical dwelling and escape burrows — Skolithos (simple vertical tube, often attributed to polychaetes), Arenicolites (U-shaped burrow), Ophiomorpha (pellet-lined burrow of callianassid shrimps); low diversity but high density; substrate is constantly reworked by waves, so organisms invest in rapid, vertical burrows as permanent homes in shifting sand; trace-makers are predominantly **suspension feeders** exploiting the food-rich, wave-agitated water column
2. **Cruziana ichnofacies**: moderate energy, subtidal shelf below fair-weather wave base; horizontal feeding and locomotion traces plus dwelling burrows — Cruziana (bilobed trilobite digging trail), Rhizocorallium (horizontal U-shaped spreite burrow of shrimps), Terebellina, Rosselia; high diversity; substrate is stable enough for complex horizontal excavation; **deposit feeders** and **suspension feeders** coexist; indicates open shelf conditions with moderate organic flux
3. **Zoophycos ichnofacies**: quiet, deeper shelf to slope, periodically oxygen-depleted; dominated by Zoophycos (helical or sheet-like spreite burrow systematically working through organic-poor sediment) and related complex feeding traces; low diversity but high abundance of Zoophycos; the organism producing Zoophycos was a systematic **deposit feeder** harvesting sediment with very low organic content; the ichnofacies indicates conditions unfavorable for suspension feeders (no active bottom currents) and for diverse bioturbators (low oxygen), but with enough oxygen to support some deposit feeding
4. **Nereites ichnofacies**: deep sea (abyssal plain, deep turbidite lobes); complex, highly patterned graphoglyptid traces (Nereites, Paleodictyon, Cosmorhaphe, Spirorhaphe) on tops of turbidite beds; high ichnodiversity; all traces are feeding structures of **systematic deposit feeders** and/or **chemosymbiotic farmers** (Paleodictyon may represent fungal gardening in tubes); the complex patterns represent optimization of area coverage in an extremely food-poor environment; graphoglyptids are almost absent from shallow environments
5. **Scoyenia ichnofacies**: continental — fluvial floodplain, alluvial plain, lacustrine margin; Scoyenia (curved, meniscate backfill burrow), Beaconites (simple horizontal burrow with backfill), vertebrate and insect tracks, root traces, desiccation-crack fills; low diversity but distinctive continental assemblage; indicates **subaerial or non-marine** depositional setting

#### Bioturbation and oxygenation
- **Bioturbation**: the physical mixing of sediment by organism activity; ranges from absent (preserving pristine lamination) to complete (destroying all physical sedimentary structures)
- **Bioturbation index (BI 0–5)**: BI 0 = no bioturbation, laminated; BI 1 = sparse, isolated burrows, <10% disturbed; BI 2 = widespread but discontinuous; BI 3 = evenly distributed, ~40% reworked; BI 4 = moderate to dense, 40–60% reworked; BI 5 = completely bioturbated, no primary sedimentary structures preserved
- **Oxygenation signal**: BI is directly related to bottom-water oxygen; BI 0–1 indicates anoxic or severely dysoxic conditions (no infaunal organisms); BI 2–3 indicates dysoxic to suboxic; BI 4–5 indicates well-oxygenated; the transition from BI 0 laminated black shale to BI 5 bioturbated mudstone in a vertical section records improving oxygenation — a direct paleoceanographic proxy

#### Precambrian–Cambrian trace record
- The oldest trace fossils are ~570–560 Ma (Ediacaran), predating the body fossil record of bilateral animals by tens of millions of years
- Ediacaran traces are simple: horizontal meandering trails (Helminthoidichnites) and shallow horizontal burrows — record soft-bodied, slow-moving, muscular animals feeding at the sediment surface; no vertical burrows, indicating soft substrate bioturbation was minimal
- The **Cambrian substrate revolution** (~540–520 Ma): the appearance of vertical burrowing (Skolithos ichnofacies) at the Precambrian–Cambrian boundary records the evolution of muscular, hard-tipped organisms capable of burrowing vertically — one of the most significant behavioral innovations in Earth history; it transformed the sediment from a firm, microbial mat-dominated surface to a bioturbated "mixed layer" — permanently changing benthic ecosystems and sediment chemistry
- Trace fossils therefore record the behavioral diversification of the earliest animals before their body fossils appear — providing a critical pre-body-fossil window into the Cambrian Explosion

### Section 4 — Quiz: 5 Questions
1. A palaeontologist finds a single trace fossil — a simple vertical cylindrical tube (Skolithos) — in a Devonian sandstone unit. Explain what this trace preserves, why it is classified as a separate ichnospecies from the organism that made it, and what environmental information it provides.
2. What are the key differences between a burrow and a boring, and what does each tell us about the substrate at the time of formation?
3. Describe the Cruziana and Skolithos ichnofacies: for each, identify (a) the typical environment, (b) the main trace types, (c) the dominant feeding strategy of trace-makers, and (d) why each assemblage characterises that environment.
4. A vertical section shows (base to top): dark laminated shale (BI 0) → grey mudstone with rare isolated burrows (BI 1–2) → bioturbated mudstone with diverse trace fossils including Thalassinoides and Ophiomorpha (BI 4–5). What does this succession record, and what is the mechanism linking bioturbation intensity to the BI change?
5. Trace fossils are found in Ediacaran rocks (~570 Ma) that predate the appearance of most animal body fossils. What can these Precambrian traces tell us about animal evolution, and what was the significance of the Cambrian appearance of vertical burrows (~540 Ma)?
