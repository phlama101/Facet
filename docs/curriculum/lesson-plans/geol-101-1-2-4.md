## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Explain what a crystal system is** — a classification of crystalline structures based on the symmetry of the repeating atomic lattice, specifically the lengths and angles of the unit cell's three axes.
2. **Name the seven crystal systems** — isometric (cubic), tetragonal, orthorhombic, hexagonal, trigonal, monoclinic, and triclinic — and describe the defining axial characteristic of each in plain language.
3. **Connect crystal system to crystal form** — explain how a mineral's internal symmetry determines its external shape, and identify the crystal system from a described or illustrated crystal form (e.g., perfect cubes → isometric; six-sided prisms → hexagonal).
4. **Connect crystal system to cleavage** — recognise that cleavage planes (introduced in 1.2.2) run parallel to the most densely packed atomic planes, which are themselves determined by the crystal system.
5. **Assign at least six familiar minerals** to their crystal systems: halite and pyrite (isometric); quartz (hexagonal); calcite (trigonal); orthoclase feldspar and gypsum (monoclinic); plagioclase feldspar (triclinic).

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "Why snowflakes have six sides"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Every snowflake has six-fold symmetry — six arms, six sides, six-sided prisms. No snowflake has five arms or eight. This is not a coincidence or an aesthetic preference of nature. It is a direct consequence of the geometry of ice's crystal lattice: the water molecules in ice organise into a hexagonal arrangement, and that internal hexagonal order expresses itself in the external shape of every crystal of ice that forms, from the largest snowflake to the smallest frost crystal. The external form of a crystal is the public face of its internal atomic geometry.

This principle applies to every crystalline mineral. The flat faces of a halite cube, the perfect rhombohedra of calcite, the six-sided prisms of quartz — all of these are external expressions of internal atomic order. Mineralogists have classified all possible crystalline arrangements into **seven crystal systems**, each defined by the symmetry of the repeating unit cell. This lesson introduces those seven systems, connects them to minerals already studied in this module, and explains why crystal form is a powerful identification tool.

**What this section establishes.**
- The snowflake example grounds the abstract concept concretely.
- The connection back to 1.2.1: crystalline structure (the lattice) was established as the fifth criterion for minerals; this lesson classifies the types of that structure.
- The connection back to 1.2.2: cleavage directions (planes of weakness) are governed by the crystal system.
- The preview that this lesson completes Module 1.2 and sets up Module 1.3.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Crystal system** — one of seven categories that classify all possible crystalline lattices by their axial symmetry. Each system is defined by the relative lengths of and angles between the three unit cell axes.
- **Unit cell** — the smallest repeating unit of a crystal lattice — the "building block" that, when stacked in three dimensions, produces the full crystal. The shape of the unit cell determines the crystal system.
- **Symmetry** — the property of a pattern that looks the same after certain transformations (rotation, reflection). A cube has higher symmetry than a brick: rotating a cube 90° around any face-to-face axis gives an identical appearance; a brick does not have this property.
- **Crystal form** — the set of geometrically equivalent faces on a crystal, determined by the crystal's internal symmetry. Perfect cubes, hexagonal prisms, octahedra, and rhombohedra are all crystal forms.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The seven systems
**Type:** `concept` (with three cards)
**Estimated reading time:** ~8–9 minutes

**Subsection A — Why classify crystal systems?** In Lesson 1.2.1 we established that minerals must have a crystalline structure — atoms in a repeating 3D lattice. In Lesson 1.2.2 we saw that cleavage planes run parallel to the most densely populated atomic planes in the lattice. The crystal system is the framework that describes *which* lattice geometry is present. All possible crystalline arrangements in nature reduce to seven geometric families — no more, no fewer. Every mineral's crystal belongs to one of these seven. Knowing a mineral's crystal system tells you what shapes its crystals will form, how many cleavage directions it will have and at what angles, and how it will interact with polarised light. At 100-level, the practical payoff is simple: crystal form is a free identification clue whenever well-formed crystals are present.

**Subsection B — The seven crystal systems.**

1. **Isometric (Cubic).** The highest-symmetry system. Three axes of equal length meeting at right angles. This three-way equality produces the maximum possible rotational symmetry: you can rotate a cubic crystal 90° around any of its three principal axes and it looks identical. Crystal forms: cubes, octahedra (8 faces), dodecahedra (12 faces). Examples: **halite** (perfect cubes — also explaining its three 90° cleavage planes), **galena** (cubes), **pyrite** (cubes, often with striated faces), **magnetite** (octahedra), **diamond** (octahedra), **fluorite** (octahedra), **garnet** (dodecahedra). The cubic cleavage of halite is a direct consequence of its isometric symmetry: the three equally-spaced cleavage planes at right angles mirror the three equal, perpendicular axes of the cubic lattice.

2. **Tetragonal.** Three axes at right angles, but one axis is longer or shorter than the other two (which remain equal). Think of a cubic system that has been stretched or compressed along one axis. Crystal forms: square prisms (like a stretched cube), square pyramids. Examples: **zircon** (square prisms with pyramidal terminations), **rutile** (TiO₂). Less commonly encountered than cubic or hexagonal in introductory geology.

3. **Orthorhombic.** Three axes at right angles, all of different lengths. No two axes are equal. Crystal forms: rectangular prisms of varying proportions. Examples: **olivine** (typically stubby, rectangular grains — the orthorhombic system explains why olivine grains lack the symmetry of cubic or hexagonal crystals), **topaz**, **sulphur**.

4. **Hexagonal.** Four axes: three of equal length in a horizontal plane meeting at 60°, and one vertical axis of different length perpendicular to them. The 60° spacing of the horizontal axes is the source of six-fold symmetry. Crystal forms: six-sided prisms, often terminated by pyramidal faces. Examples: **quartz** (the classic hexagonal prism with pyramidal tip), **beryl** (emerald and aquamarine), **apatite**. This is also the system of **ice** — the direct explanation for the six-fold symmetry of snowflakes.

5. **Trigonal (Rhombohedral).** Often treated as a subdivision of the hexagonal system because it uses the same four-axis description, but has three-fold rather than six-fold symmetry. Crystal forms: rhombohedra (skewed cubes), scalenohedra. Examples: **calcite** (rhombohedra — explaining its three rhombohedral cleavage planes), **dolomite**, **tourmaline**, **quartz** (which actually belongs to the trigonal system despite its hexagonal external appearance, due to its three-fold rotational symmetry). For 100-level purposes, hexagonal and trigonal are often grouped together under "hexagonal."

6. **Monoclinic.** Three axes of unequal length; two meet at right angles, but the third is oblique. The single oblique angle gives this system its name (mono = one, clinic = inclined). This produces crystals with two-fold symmetry and often distinctly asymmetric prism shapes. Crystal forms: prisms with slanted end faces. Examples: **orthoclase feldspar** (explaining the slightly non-90° angles sometimes observed on cleavage faces), **biotite** and **muscovite** mica, **hornblende** (amphibole), **augite** (pyroxene), **gypsum**. Monoclinic is actually the most common crystal system — a large fraction of all known mineral species crystallise in it.

7. **Triclinic.** Three axes of unequal length, none meeting at right angles. No right angles anywhere. This is the lowest-symmetry system — only one-fold symmetry (meaning only a full 360° rotation returns the crystal to its original appearance). Crystal forms: irregular, typically tabular or bladed. Examples: **plagioclase feldspar** (the striations on cleavage faces are related to its triclinic symmetry and twin planes), **kyanite**, **turquoise**. Despite low symmetry, triclinic minerals can still have well-defined cleavage — plagioclase, like orthoclase, has two cleavage planes, but they intersect at angles slightly different from 90°, which is the observable consequence of the triclinic lattice.

**Subsection C — Crystal system → cleavage: the connection.** In Lesson 1.2.2, we defined cleavage as breakage along planes of weaker bonds in the crystal lattice. The crystal system governs where those planes are. In the isometric system, three mutually perpendicular sets of planes are equally well-developed, producing three cleavage directions at 90° (halite, galena). In the hexagonal/trigonal system, specific planes parallel to the c-axis or basal plane dominate, producing the characteristic rhombohedral cleavage of calcite. In the monoclinic system, one oblique axis means cleavage planes may intersect at slightly non-90° angles — which is why augite (pyroxene, monoclinic) and hornblende (amphibole, monoclinic) both have two cleavage planes, but the angles are slightly different, and this angle difference (90° for pyroxene vs. 60°/120° for amphibole) is ultimately rooted in subtle differences in their monoclinic lattice parameters. Understanding crystal systems makes the cleavage rules memorable rather than arbitrary.

**Subsection D — How to use crystal form in the field.** When well-formed crystals are present, crystal form is often the fastest identification shortcut:
- Perfect cubes → isometric: halite, galena, pyrite, fluorite
- Six-sided prisms with pyramidal tips → hexagonal/trigonal: quartz, beryl
- Flat rhombohedral fragments → trigonal: calcite
- Thin, flexible sheets → monoclinic sheet structure: mica
- Irregular bladed or tabular crystals → monoclinic or triclinic: feldspars

When crystals are not well-formed (which is common in most rocks, where minerals crystallise in competition with neighbours), the physical property tests from 1.2.2 take over.

**Module 1.2 close.** This lesson completes Module 1.2 — Minerals: The Building Blocks. We have moved from defining what a mineral is (1.2.1) to identifying minerals by their physical properties (1.2.2) to meeting the specific minerals that build Earth's rocks (1.2.3) to classifying the geometric basis for all of those properties (1.2.4). ## 3. Key terms introduced

| Term | Definition planned |
|---|---|
| Crystal system | One of seven categories classifying all crystalline lattices by axial symmetry; determined by the relative lengths and angles of the unit cell axes. |
| Unit cell | The smallest repeating building block of a crystal lattice; its shape determines the crystal system. |
| Symmetry | The property of looking the same after rotations or reflections; isometric has highest symmetry; triclinic has lowest. |
| Crystal form | The set of geometrically equivalent faces on a crystal; determined by internal symmetry. |
| Isometric (cubic) | Three equal axes at right angles; highest symmetry; forms cubes, octahedra; examples: halite, pyrite, diamond. |
| Hexagonal | Three equal horizontal axes at 60° + one perpendicular; six-fold symmetry; forms six-sided prisms; examples: quartz, beryl, ice. |
| Trigonal | Three-fold symmetry subdivision of hexagonal; forms rhombohedra; examples: calcite, dolomite, tourmaline. |
| Monoclinic | Three unequal axes, one oblique; most common system by number of species; examples: orthoclase, mica, augite, gypsum. |
| Triclinic | Three unequal axes, no right angles; lowest symmetry; examples: plagioclase, kyanite. |

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"Why does every snowflake have six sides — is that just a coincidence?"**
   *Resolved by:* The intro hook and the hexagonal crystal system description. It is not a coincidence: ice belongs to the hexagonal system, whose lattice has six-fold symmetry, which every crystal of ice must express externally.

2. **"How does the inside of a crystal control what the outside looks like?"**
   *Resolved by:* The unit cell concept and the system-by-system examples. The unit cell geometry determines the symmetry; the symmetry determines which faces can form and at what angles; those faces are the external crystal form.

3. **"Why does calcite always cleave into the same rhombohedral shape — where does that shape come from?"**
   *Resolved by:* The trigonal system section and the crystal-system-to-cleavage connection. Calcite's trigonal lattice has three sets of atomic planes at specific angles; those planes are where bonds are weakest; cleavage follows those planes; the result is always the same rhombohedron.
