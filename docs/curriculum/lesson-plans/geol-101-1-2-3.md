## 1. Learning objectives

Upon completing this lesson, the student will be able to:

1. **Explain why silicate minerals dominate Earth's crust and mantle** — silicon and oxygen are the two most abundant elements in the crust; the silica tetrahedron (SiO₄) is the fundamental structural unit from which all silicate minerals are built.
2. **Identify and describe the six main rock-forming silicate mineral groups** — quartz, feldspar (orthoclase and plagioclase), mica (muscovite and biotite), pyroxene, amphibole, and olivine — with their key identification properties and the rocks/settings where each is found.
3. **Name the most important non-silicate mineral groups** — carbonates (calcite, dolomite), oxides (hematite, magnetite), sulphides (pyrite, galena), and evaporites (halite, gypsum) — with one diagnostic property and one occurrence for each.
4. **Connect mineral assemblages to rock types and Earth layers** — recognising that feldspar + quartz + mica → granite (continental crust); pyroxene + olivine → basalt (oceanic crust / upper mantle); calcite → limestone (sedimentary); and so on.

## 2. Section breakdown

### Section 1 of 3 — Intro: "A short list for a whole planet"
**Type:** `intro`
**Estimated reading time:** ~3 minutes

**Hook.** Earth's crust contains roughly 5,500 known mineral species. Yet the vast majority of all crustal rock — granite, basalt, limestone, sandstone, shale — is built from fewer than 30 of them. And if you narrow further to the minerals that make up most of the crust by volume, you get a list of perhaps a dozen. This lesson is about that short list: the rock-forming minerals that underpin everything geology studies at the surface.

**What this section establishes.**
- The scale of the task: ~5,500 species exist, but ~30 do most of the work.
- The reason for silicate dominance: silicon and oxygen are the first and second most abundant elements in Earth's crust.
- The connection forward: being able to recognise these minerals by the properties studied in 1.2.2 is the practical skill that enables rock identification throughout the rest of the course.
- Key vocabulary for the concept section.

**Key terms introduced:**
- **Silicate mineral** — a mineral whose fundamental structural unit is the silica tetrahedron: one silicon atom bonded to four oxygen atoms (SiO₄). Silicates are the most abundant mineral group by far, making up ~90% of Earth's crust.
- **Silica tetrahedron** — the SiO₄ unit: a silicon atom surrounded by four oxygen atoms at the corners of a tetrahedron. These units link together in different ways to form the different silicate mineral families.
- **Mafic minerals** — silicate minerals rich in magnesium and iron (olivine, pyroxene, amphibole); dark-coloured, relatively dense. The term was introduced in Lesson 1.1.2; here it is applied at the mineral level.
- **Felsic minerals** — silicate minerals rich in silicon and aluminium (quartz, feldspar, most mica); light-coloured, lower density. Also introduced in 1.1.2; applied at the mineral level here.

### Section 2 of 3 — Concept: The minerals that build the planet
**Type:** `concept` (with three cards)
**Estimated reading time:** ~9–10 minutes

**Subsection A — Why silicon and oxygen?** Earth's crust is approximately 46% oxygen and 28% silicon by mass — together nearly three quarters of crustal composition. Oxygen and silicon bond strongly and form the **silica tetrahedron** (SiO₄): one silicon surrounded by four oxygen atoms. These tetrahedra can link to each other in different ways — sharing corners, edges, or forming isolated units — producing the structural diversity of the silicate mineral family. Every silicate mineral, from the simplest olivine to the complex micas, is built from variations of this one unit. The dominance of silicates in the crust and mantle is simply the consequence of silicon and oxygen being so overwhelmingly abundant.

**Subsection B — The six silicate groups.**

1. **Quartz (SiO₂)** — The only common mineral made entirely of silicon and oxygen, with all tetrahedra fully linked. Hardness 7, no cleavage (conchoidal fracture), vitreous luster, white streak. One of the most abundant minerals in continental crust; makes up most of the sand on Earth's beaches. Chemically very stable — resists weathering better than almost any other mineral, which is why it concentrates in sand and sandstone.

2. **Feldspar group** — The single most abundant mineral group in Earth's crust, making up roughly 60% of the crust by volume. All feldspars are framework silicates with two cleavage planes at nearly right angles (blocky fragments), hardness ~6, and vitreous luster. Two main varieties: **orthoclase** (K-feldspar: KAlSi₃O₈) is typically pink or white and is the feldspar of granite and continental crust; **plagioclase** (Na/Ca feldspar) is typically white to grey and is the dominant feldspar in basalt and oceanic crust. Distinguishing them: plagioclase commonly shows fine parallel striations (striping) on cleavage surfaces due to twinning; orthoclase does not.

3. **Mica group** — Sheet silicates with one perfect cleavage plane, allowing them to be peeled into thin, flexible sheets. Two main varieties: **muscovite** (white/silver mica: KAl₂(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂) — colourless to silvery, found in granites and metamorphic rocks; **biotite** (black/brown mica: K(Mg,Fe)₃(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂) — dark, found in granites and many igneous rocks. Both have hardness ~2–3. The sheet structure gives mica its characteristic pearly luster and flexible cleavage sheets.

4. **Pyroxene group** — Single-chain silicates; dark green to black, hardness ~5–6, two cleavage planes at ~90°. **Augite** is the most common pyroxene. Found in basalt, gabbro, and the lower lithosphere. A key mafic mineral — its presence signals mafic or ultramafic rock.

5. **Amphibole group** — Double-chain silicates; similar appearance to pyroxenes (dark, hard) but with two cleavage planes at ~60°/120° rather than ~90° — this cleavage angle is the primary field distinction between pyroxene and amphibole. **Hornblende** is the most common amphibole. Found in granite, diorite, and many metamorphic rocks.

6. **Olivine ((Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄)** — Isolated tetrahedra (nesosilicate); glassy green, hardness ~6.5–7, no cleavage (conchoidal to irregular fracture). The dominant mineral of the upper mantle; also found in basalt and gabbro. Olivine is a high-temperature mineral: it crystallises early from cooling magma, before most other silicates. Its presence in a rock is a strong indicator of a mafic or ultramafic composition. The connection to Lesson 1.1.2: the "mafic minerals" of oceanic crust are primarily pyroxene, plagioclase, and olivine.

**Subsection C — Connecting silicates to rocks and Earth layers.**
- **Continental crust (granite)**: quartz + orthoclase feldspar + muscovite + biotite → the classic speckled appearance of granite
- **Oceanic crust (basalt)**: plagioclase + pyroxene (augite) + olivine → dark, fine-grained basalt
- **Upper mantle**: olivine + pyroxene (dominates peridotite, the rock of the uppermost mantle)
- **Metamorphic rocks**: micas and amphiboles are characteristic of medium-to-high grade metamorphism

**Subsection D — The non-silicate minerals.** Not every mineral is a silicate. The non-silicate groups are less abundant in the crust by volume, but they include many economically important minerals and several with distinctive and diagnostic properties.

- **Carbonates** — contain CO₃. **Calcite (CaCO₃)**: hardness 3, three rhombohedral cleavage planes, white or colourless, vitreous to pearly, fizzes vigorously in dilute hydrochloric acid — the single most diagnostic chemical test in field geology. The dominant mineral of limestone and marble. **Dolomite (CaMg(CO₃)₂)**: similar to calcite but fizzes only in powdered form or warm acid. Widespread in ancient sedimentary sequences.

- **Oxides** — oxygen bonded to metal(s). **Hematite (Fe₂O₃)**: red-brown streak (the definitive test), metallic to earthy luster, hardness ~5–6. The most important iron ore mineral and the source of the red colour in red sandstones and soils. **Magnetite (Fe₃O₄)**: black, strongly magnetic (will pick up a compass needle or iron filings), hardness ~5.5–6.5. Common in igneous and metamorphic rocks; an important ore.

- **Sulphides** — sulphur bonded to metal. **Pyrite (FeS₂)**: pale brass-yellow, metallic, cubic crystals, hardness ~6–6.5, greenish-black streak — the "fool's gold" of lore. Extremely common; forms in many geological settings. **Galena (PbS)**: bright metallic luster, perfect cubic cleavage, very high density (SG ~7.6), grey streak. The primary ore of lead.

- **Evaporites / halides** — minerals that precipitate from evaporating water. **Halite (NaCl)**: cubic cleavage (perfect cubes), salty taste, hardness ~2.5. Forms in evaporating seas and inland lakes. **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)**: hardness ~2 (scratched by a fingernail), white or colourless, vitreous to silky luster. Forms thick sequences in evaporite basins; the mineral from which plaster is made.

### Section 3 of 3 — Quiz
**Type:** `quiz`
**Question count:** 3

Question topics planned:
1. **Silicate dominance** — why do silicate minerals make up ~90% of Earth's crust? (silicon and oxygen are the two most abundant crustal elements; the silica tetrahedron is the fundamental building unit; distractors propose incorrect reasons)
2. **Mineral-to-rock connection** — which mineral assemblage is most consistent with a piece of granite? (quartz + orthoclase feldspar + mica — the classic felsic assemblage; distractors mix in mafic minerals inconsistent with granite)
3. **Diagnostic field test** — a rock fizzes vigorously when a drop of dilute acid is applied to it; which mineral is almost certainly responsible? (calcite — the acid-carbonate reaction is highly diagnostic; distractors confuse with other minerals)

## 5. Three concept questions this lesson will answer

1. **"Why is everything basically quartz and feldspar? Why those minerals?"**
   *Resolved by:* The silicon-oxygen dominance explanation. Si and O are 74% of the crust by mass; of course the minerals built from them dominate. Feldspar tops the list because it accommodates the widest range of common cation substitutions within the silicate framework.

2. **"What do the minerals in a rock actually tell you about it?"**
   *Resolved by:* The rock-mineral connection table. Mineral assemblage is a geological fingerprint: quartz + orthoclase + mica = granite = felsic = continental; plagioclase + pyroxene + olivine = basalt = mafic = oceanic; calcite alone = limestone = carbonate sediment.

3. **"How do field geologists quickly identify common minerals without a lab?"**
   *Resolved by:* Applying the 1.2.2 toolkit to specific minerals: quartz (hardness 7 + conchoidal fracture), calcite (hardness 3 + fizzes in acid), pyrite (metallic gold + greenish-black streak), feldspar (two cleavage planes at 90°), mica (perfect single cleavage → sheets).
