## CRITICAL Issues

These four issues have the highest probability of leaving a first-time reader confused or mis-informed. Fix these first.

---

### C-1 · `volc-101-1-2-3` — Term introduced, never defined

**Criterion 2 — Term Introduction**

The term **glass transition** is bolded in prose — signalling formal introduction — but is never defined anywhere in the lesson: not in the key-terms list, not in the card, not in an annotation.

> "When the strain rate imposed by decompression exceeds a critical threshold (~0.01 s⁻¹ for typical silicic melts), the melt crosses the **glass transition** and fractures…"

A reader encountering this for the first time has no way to know what "glass transition" means physically. The concept is the entire explanation for explosive fragmentation, so leaving it undefined makes the following paragraph incoherent.

**Fix:** Add to key-terms:
```
{ term: 'Glass Transition', def: 'The threshold at which a melt switches from ductile (deformable) to brittle (fracturing) behaviour. Above this strain rate the melt cannot flow fast enough to accommodate deformation, so it snaps instead of stretching.' }
```

---

### C-2 · `ocea-101-1-1-2` — Callout introduces unrelated fact

**Criterion 4 — Intro Duplication / Callout Contract**

The intro builds around mid-ocean ridges and seafloor spreading. The callout headline reads "Ocean salt could bury every continent" — a fact about total dissolved salt volume that has no connection to the lesson's subject. This is not duplication; it is active misdirection. A reader who read the intro and then encounters the callout wonders whether they are still in the same lesson.

**Fix:** Replace with a callout that deepens the intro narrative. Candidate: *Hydrothermal vents cycle the entire volume of Earth's oceans through the seafloor every 8–10 million years* — a fact already present in the concept section that would work well as a hook.

---

### C-3 · `ocea-101-1-1-4` — "Milankovitch cycles" undefined in card

**Criterion 2 — Jargon in cards without introduction**

The `δ¹⁸O and Climate Records` card's description names Milankovitch cycles and gives their periods without explaining what they are:

> "Orbital cycles drive the pattern (Milankovitch cycles: 100 kyr, 41 kyr, 23 kyr)."

A reader who reads cards independently (many do) gets numbers with no physical meaning. The lesson prose does not define the term either.

**Fix:** Expand the card line to:
> "Orbital cycles (Milankovitch variations: changes in Earth's orbital eccentricity ~100 kyr, axial tilt ~41 kyr, and wobble ~23 kyr) drive the pattern of glacial and interglacial periods recorded in the isotope signal."

---

### C-4 · `ocea-101-1-2-4` — Tsunami section opens with a negation, not a claim

**Criterion 5 — Concept Section Structure**

The tsunamis subsection begins: *"Despite their colloquial name 'tidal waves,' tsunamis have nothing to do with tides — they are seismic sea waves generated by submarine earthquakes…"* The opener corrects a misconception before establishing what tsunamis actually are. Technical details follow immediately. A reader who does not already have a mental model for "seismic sea wave" is then asked to track wavelengths, speeds, and shoaling without a foundation.

**Fix:** Reorder into the standard three-part structure:

1. **Core claim:** "Tsunamis are seismic sea waves produced by sudden vertical displacement of the seafloor — most often from a megathrust earthquake."
2. **Mechanism:** "In the deep ocean they travel at ~800 km/h with wavelengths of 100–500 km…"
3. **So what:** "When they reach shallow coastal water, they slow to ~30 km/h and their energy compresses vertically, driving the dramatic wave heights that make tsunamis so destructive."

Then correct the misconception as a parenthetical in step 1.

## MODERATE Issues

These issues produce friction — a reader can follow but must work harder than necessary. Fixes are mostly paragraph splits or reorderings.

---

### M-1 · `geol-101-1-2-3` — Three new terms in one paragraph

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

A single paragraph introduces silicate rock classification, the mafic/felsic spectrum, and mineral-level vocabulary (olivine, pyroxene, quartz, feldspars) without separating them.

**Fix:** Split into two paragraphs: (1) the mafic–felsic spectrum as a concept, (2) which minerals define each end.

---

### M-2 · `ocea-101-1-1-3` — Megathrust paragraph overloaded

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

One paragraph introduces three distinct concepts: the subduction thrust interface and locking mechanism, elastic rebound, and tsunami generation conditions.

> "The interface between the subducting plate and the overriding plate is called the **subduction thrust**… When the lock suddenly fails, the overriding plate springs back upward and seaward in what is called **elastic rebound**. If the fault ruptures over a large area… generating both seismic waves and… a **tsunami**…"

**Fix:** Split into three focused paragraphs, one per concept.

---

### M-3 · `ocea-101-1-1-4` — Acronyms in cards without definition

**Criterion 2 — Jargon in cards**

The `Deep-Sea Resources` card uses **REEs** (rare earth elements) and **UNCLOS** without spelling them out anywhere in the lesson.

**Fix:** Spell out on first use in the card: "…high Co, Pt, and REEs (rare earth elements)…" and "…developing international seabed law (UNCLOS — the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea)."

---

### M-4 · `ocea-101-1-2-2` — Water masses section omits core claim

**Criterion 5 — Concept Section Structure**

The "Water masses" subsection jumps directly to examples (NADW, AABW) without first stating what a water mass is and why the concept matters.

**Fix:** Open with: "A **water mass** is a body of ocean water with a characteristic temperature–salinity combination that records where and when it sank from the surface. Because this signature is preserved for centuries, water masses act as tracers of deep-ocean circulation." Then provide examples.

---

### M-5 · `seis-101-1-1-2` — Snell's Law definition is equation-first

**Criterion 2 — Jargon in key-terms**

The key-term definition leads with the equation `sin(i₁)/v₁ = sin(i₂)/v₂` before providing physical meaning. A first-time reader without maths background encounters notation before understanding.

**Fix:** Lead with plain language: "Snell's Law describes how seismic waves bend at a velocity boundary — the greater the velocity contrast, the sharper the bend. Mathematically: sin(i₁)/v₁ = sin(i₂)/v₂, where i is angle from vertical and v is wave speed."

---

### M-6 · `atmo-101-1-1-2` — Water vapour paragraph has three sub-concepts

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

A single paragraph covers: water vapour as the most abundant greenhouse gas; the feedback-vs.-forcing distinction; and the Clausius-Clapeyron relationship.

**Fix:** Split into two paragraphs: (1) water vapour as a powerful GHG, (2) why it is a feedback rather than a forcing, explained through Clausius-Clapeyron.

---

### M-7 · `atmo-101-1-3-2` — Hail paragraph dense with multiple mechanisms

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

The hail formation paragraph covers: initiation as a frozen droplet, lofting, supercooled water accretion, layer-by-layer growth, record hailstone dimensions, and updraft strength required — all without breaks.

**Fix:** Split at the natural seam: (1) hail formation process, (2) record hailstones and the updraft physics that produce extreme sizes.

---

### M-8 · `atmo-101-1-3-4` — Observing system paragraph is a list in disguise

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

The paragraph on the global observing system enumerates five independent components (radiosondes, satellites, radar, aircraft, surface stations) inside a single paragraph without a governing concept unifying them.

**Fix:** Add a structuring sentence and either split by altitude tier (high / mid / surface) or use a short introductory sentence followed by a brief itemised list within the paragraph.

---

### M-9 · `atmo-101-1-4-1` — "OHC" abbreviation in card

**Criterion 2 — Jargon in cards**

The WISHE Intensification Loop card uses the abbreviation **OHC** without spelling it out in the card itself.

> "OHC >50 kJ/cm² strongly linked to rapid intensification."

**Fix:** Use full term in the card: "Ocean heat content (OHC) >50 kJ/cm² is strongly linked to rapid intensification."

### M-10 · `atmo-101-1-4-4` — Freezing rain layers numbered but not separated

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

The three atmospheric layers required for freezing rain are numbered (1), (2), (3) within a single paragraph, defeating the structural signal the numbers imply.

**Fix:** Provide each layer its own sentence or sentence-pair in the paragraph, with an explicit topic sentence: "Freezing rain requires three distinct temperature layers stacked vertically."

### M-11 · `volc-101-1-1-4` — Calderas section lacks opening claim

**Criterion 5 — Concept Section Structure**

The calderas concept section opens with a definition rather than a claim. A reader finishing this section cannot easily state *why calderas matter* or what the surprising insight is.

**Fix:** Open with a claim: "Calderas form not from eruption alone but from collapse — and some of the world's most dangerous volcanic systems are not mountains but holes in the ground." Then provide definition and mechanism.

### M-12 · `volc-101-1-3-1` — Lahar physics paragraph overloaded

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

A single paragraph introduces the Bingham plastic rheology, yield strength, viscous flow behaviour, and velocity scaling — three conceptually distinct physical ideas.

**Fix:** Split into two paragraphs: (1) what makes lahars different from water flows (Bingham plastic behaviour), (2) how yield strength and viscosity control velocity and runout.

### M-13 · `hyd-101-1-1-2` — Precipitation and ET combined

**Criterion 1 — One Concept Per Paragraph**

The lesson combines precipitation mechanisms, the Penman-Monteith equation for evapotranspiration, and the water balance equation within a section that does not give each concept independent paragraph treatment.

**Fix:** Give each concept its own paragraph: (1) precipitation (formation, types), (2) evapotranspiration (what it is, Penman-Monteith in one sentence), (3) the water balance equation as the synthesis.

### M-14 · `geom-101-1-1-2` — Intro and callout duplicate Huascarán details

**Criterion 4 — Intro Duplication**

Both the intro and the callout describe the 1970 Nevado Huascarán event with nearly identical statistics (180 km travel, high speed, ~20,000 deaths).

**Fix:** Callout should not restate the intro example. Replace with downstream content: consequences for slope risk mapping, or what the Huascarán case revealed about long-runout mechanisms.

### M-15 · `geom-101-1-4-2` — Oso landslide figures in both intro and card

**Criterion 4 — Intro Duplication**

The 2014 Oso, Washington landslide is described in the intro (rotational → debris flow transformation, 43 deaths, 8 km runout) and again in the card examples with the same statistics.

**Fix:** Card should focus on the mechanism (rotational-to-flow transformation, pore-pressure role) without repeating the casualty and distance figures already given in the intro.

### Pattern B — Takeaway sections paraphrase rather than crystallise (~7 instances)

Affected lessons: `geol-101-1-3-1`, `atmo-101-1-1-4`, `atmo-101-1-3-3`, `volc-101-1-1-2`, `ocea-101-1-2-2`.

Takeaways use nearly identical phrasing to the concept section, defeating their purpose. A takeaway should lift the reader to a higher level of abstraction — not restate what they just read.

**Example from `atmo-101-1-3-3`:**
- Concept says: "An unsaturated parcel is cooler than the environment (stable); conditional instability is the setup for deep convection…"
- Takeaway says: "Conditionally unstable (6 < ELR < 10°C/km): stable if unsaturated, unstable if saturated and forced to LFC…"

**Pattern fix:** Rewrite each takeaway starting from the reader's perspective — what can they now explain or predict that they couldn't before? Takeaway language should not repeat any complete phrase from the concept section.

---

### Pattern C — Transitions absent between major sections (~9 instances)

Affected lessons: `geol-101-1-3-x` (convection → heat sources), `atmo-101-1-1-3` (latitudinal imbalance → solar spectrum), `atmo-101-1-3-1` (cloud genera → flat cloud bases), `ocea-101-1-1-3` (megathrust → deep-ocean pressure), `ocea-101-1-1-4` (sediment types → CCD), `seis-101-1-1-2` (Moho → shadow zones).

**Pattern:** The last sentence of one section ends a thought; the next section opens a new thought. There is no sentence bridging them.

**Pattern fix:** Write a single bridge sentence at the end of the paragraph or as the opening of the next one. It should either signal what comes next ("But seismology is not the only evidence…") or ask the question the next paragraph answers ("If the mantle flows, why do earthquakes happen there at all?").

---

### Pattern D — Dense card descriptions (~6 instances)

Affected cards: `geom-101-1-1-1` (4 weathering types in one sentence), `ocea-101-1-1-4` (Deep-Sea Resources), `seis-101-1-1-2` (Snell's Law equation), `atmo-101-1-4-1` (WISHE card).

**Pattern:** Cards pack multiple mechanisms or technical terms into 1–2 sentences, exceeding the "plain language" standard.

**Pattern fix:** Move subsidiary mechanisms to the `examples` field. The `desc` should answer "what is this?" in plain language; `examples` can add depth.

---

### Pattern E — Simple concepts slightly over-explained (~4 instances)

Affected: `geol-101-1-1-1` (Kola Borehole hook ~200 words), `atmo-101-1-3-1` (cloud formation intro, thorough but lengthy), `hyd-101-1-1-1` (hydrological cycle intro).

These are not failures — they are well-written — but the motivation section uses more words than the concept's importance warrants. Space given to motivating material may be better spent on more complex downstream concepts.

**Pattern fix:** Trim motivating paragraphs to their essential hook (2–3 sentences). The reader who is already engaged does not need to be convinced longer.

## Strengths Observed Across the Curriculum

These principles are applied well and should be preserved:

1. **Term introduction discipline** — ~95% of technical terms are defined at or before first use in prose. This is the hardest rule to maintain at scale and it is mostly working.

2. **Concrete card examples** — Cards anchor concepts to real places and numbers ("Guilin limestone towers," "Victoria Falls," "Ibyuk Pingo in the Mackenzie Delta"). This is exactly right.

3. **Quiz explanation quality** — Explanations address why wrong answers are wrong, not just confirming the right one. This is consistently strong.

4. **Paragraph-level topic discipline** — The "one concept per paragraph" rule is followed in the vast majority of cases. Violations are specific and correctable.

5. **Takeaway uniqueness (most lessons)** — Most lessons crystallise at a higher level rather than copy-pasting. The exceptions noted above are the minority.
